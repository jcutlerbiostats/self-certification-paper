# 1. Introduction

Almost everyone thinks it about themselves. Ask a scientist, a journalist, a partisan or even a dogmatist of any description whether they follow the evidence where it leads, and the answer is almost always yes — sincerely and often without hesitation. Indeed, how many of us can say we've ever heard an admission to the contrary — an admission of intellectual dishonesty? The claim is so nearly universal that its negation is a remarkable thing to hear. 

And yet, no one has ever been able to check it. What's more, asserting it about oneself is ironically contrary to the practice of intellectual honesty. 

That contradiction and my proposed solution are the subject of this paper. **It is not a complaint about hypocrisy**. My argument does not depend on anyone lying. It is not weakened by supposing that every person who has ever claimed to follow the evidence was reporting the truth as they understood it. What this paper attempts to show is that the self-reported claim carries no information and is self-defeating regardless — that it would be uninformative and indefensible even in a population of scrupulous, sincere reporters — **and that this is a fact about the structure of the claim rather than about the character of the people making it**. 

Often the diagnosis runs the other way. The problem is that people are tribalistic, motivated reasoners, or poorly educated, and the remedy is **exhortation and instruction**: teach critical thinking, model intellectual humility, give people tools. We have pursued that remedy with considerable resources for over a century, and the results are instructive in a way the standard account cannot explain. Instruction produces real, measurable gains on tests of critical thinking,[^ct] yet the gains transfer poorly out of the classroom, and they are deployed least of all where they matter most — on the reasoner's own identity-laden beliefs. 

---

[^ct]: Abrami et al. (2015) find a mean effect of g+ ≈ 0.30 across 341 effect sizes — instruction works, on the instrument. On the failure of transfer, Willingham (2008): critical thinking is not a domain-general skill but is context-dependent, which is why classroom gains do not follow the student out of the classroom.

---

The capacity is teachable. What has never been **incentivized** is its application to one's core beliefs, and there is a better explanation for that than lazy students or stubborn adults: the remedy has been aimed at the wrong thing. The binding constraint was never that people struggle to reason well. Most can identify a fallacy in an opponent's argument easily enough. It is that nothing has ever attached to deploying that capacity against oneself, **because there is no objective way for others to tell whether that is what you are doing**. Whether you end up agreeing with them is far more visible, and far too distracting. 

That absence is what this paper attempts to characterize precisely. §2 sets out the phenomenon: a claim to intellectual honesty that almost everyone makes, at least implicitly, that carries real consequences for how credibility is allocated, and that no one has ever been in a position to verify. §3 argues that the claim is not merely difficult to verify but structurally uninformative and self-defeating — that self-reported intellectual honesty cannot in principle supply evidence of the practice, **and that the claim, made on one's own authority, instantiates the very failure it denies** — and that a record of one's own corrections does not repair this. §4 takes up the obvious reply, that we already have institutions for this. §5 asks what an adequate check would have to be, and derives the requirements from the diagnosis rather than from any existing institution. §6 draws a corollary that I take to be the paper's main direct consequence. 

So far as I have been able to determine, the question has not been addressed. Virtue epistemology has produced careful accounts of what the intellectual virtues *are* — honesty and humility as states of character among them — and the epistemology of testimony has produced careful accounts of when to accept what others tell us about the world.[^gap] What neither literature takes up is the case in which the testimony concerns the person's own epistemic conduct: what could warrant accepting a person's claim to intellectual honesty, and what an attribution of it would have to rest on to be valid. That gap is this paper's subject. 

---

[^gap]: For the character-trait treatments, see Whitcomb, Battaly, Baehr, and Howard-Snyder (2017) on intellectual humility, Roberts and Wood (2007) on the intellectual virtues generally, and Miller (2021) on honesty; for testimony, the surveys collected in the standard bibliographies. One conflation to head off: Miller's subject is honesty as a *moral* virtue — reliably not distorting the facts as one takes them to be. That concerns the relation between belief and assertion. The practice at issue here concerns the relation between evidence and belief, and a person could possess Miller's virtue in full while never once updating anything. I say "so far as I have been able to determine" advisedly — the claim rests on searches, not on a proof of absence.

---

The nearest existing treatment is the literature on the paradoxes of humility, where it is argued that asserting *"I am humble"* undermines itself by revealing attitudes incompatible with the virtue claimed. The argument below is that paradox's harder relative: what defeats a claim to intellectual honesty is not what the assertion *reveals about the speaker's attitudes* but what it *reserves* — the authority to decide when an objection to one's own epistemic conduct or to an individual truth proposition or argument has landed. And the principle that no one may hold that authority is not new. *Nemo iudex in causa sua* — no one may be judge in their own cause — has been a fixture of natural justice since Roman law, applied without controversy to tribunals, audits, and licensure. What follows examines the one place it has never been applied: the most frequently made claim (even if implicit) about epistemic conduct there is.

Two clarifications about scope: 

First, I am concerned throughout with **attributions and practice**, not with dispositions. The question is not whether anyone possesses the inner virtue of intellectual honesty, but whether the attribution of the *practice* to a person can be warranted, and by what. I take intellectual honesty to be something one does, in the way that a person swims regularly, rather than something one has; §3.6 says why this matters. The reader who prefers a dispositional account (Roberts and Wood 2007; Whitcomb et al. 2017) will find that the argument survives, since it concerns what would warrant *attributing* the trait, whichever analysis of the trait is correct. §5.2 makes this explicit.

I do commit to one constraint on the analyses available, and I state it here rather than let it emerge unannounced. Whatever else intellectual honesty consists in, it cannot be compatible with closedness to the possibility that one is wrong — for reasons of one's self-consistency. This is a constraint on the space of admissible accounts rather than an account of my own; it rules out a class without selecting a member, and I take it to be one that a virtue epistemologist and a practice theorist can accept on the same terms. §7.1 says what secures it. 

Second, I claim nothing about anyone's blameworthiness, and the argument in fact runs the other way. If mere self-reported or self-monitored intellectual honesty can only be self-defeating (as I will attempt to show), and no institution has ever happened to supply the conditions to make the attribution coherently defensible either, then everyone who has ever asserted their own (aspiration to) intellectual honesty has been doing the only thing available, at no fault of their own. The universality of the self-reported claim and the universality of its unverifiability are the same fact, from two different angles. If a claim can't be checked, nothing stops anyone from making it. So everyone does. 

# 2. The self-certification problem

Consider what happens when someone's intellectual honesty is at issue. 

It is at issue constantly. Public disagreement is not, for the most part, a dispute about the same set of first-order facts that the participants have carefully weighed together and yet somehow read differently. It is a dispute about who is reasoning honestly and who is not. That is why the accusation is so common (even if only implied), in both directions, and why it is so rarely resolved. Each party takes the other to be motivated in their reasoning; each takes themselves to be following the evidence; and there is no procedure whatever in public discourse for settling the matter. 

What is sincerely offered in place of a procedure is testimony, from the person whose conduct is in question. 

I will call this **self-certification**: the practice, universal because nothing else is available, of an agent supplying the evidence for their own virtuous epistemic conduct. It takes three forms. Most commonly, bare assertion — *I follow the evidence; I have no agenda (or if I have one, I won't let it get in the way); I would change my mind if the evidence demanded it*. Second, a record of past corrections, offered as demonstration. Third, an appeal to method: *I am a scientist, I hold myself to the standards of my field* — which is a claim about one's own conformity to a standard, and is therefore similarly self-supplied. 

The consequences are not academic. Credibility is allocated on the persuasiveness of these self-certifications (even if merely implied) in the minds of different audiences. Credibility then determines collective behavior and the structuring of power from the smallest to the largest scales in society. Where the certifications are unverifiable and mutually contradictory — as they always are, since both parties issue them and only issue them when there is a disagreement — allocation falls back on the only remaining signals available: prior agreement, group membership, institutional affiliation, and confidence of delivery. Whatever else those are, they are not measures of epistemic conduct. 

That self-certification is unreliable is a familiar complaint and not mine. People are poor judges of their own reasoning; the literature on that is large and I take it as read.[^judges] My claim is stronger and different in kind: self-certification is uninformative and structurally self-defeating, and would remain so even if everyone issuing it were both sincere and careful. Reporter quality is not the variable that has failed. 

---
[^judges]: Miller (2021, part II) reviews the empirical psychology of honesty-relevant behavior; the picture is not encouraging even for the moral virtue, let alone for self-assessment of one's reasoning. 
---

That is the argument of the next section. 

# 3. Why self-certification is structurally uninformative and self-defeating 

## 3.1 Two claims, distinguished

§2 separated the familiar complaint from mine. The familiar complaint is that self-reported intellectual honesty is **unreliable**: the signal is often wrong. If that were the whole problem, the remedy would be more rigorous introspection or a stronger norm of self-awareness, and the problem would be difficult but not structural. 

Mine is that self-reported intellectual honesty is **structurally defective**, and in two ways. It is **uninformative**: the signal carries no information about the underlying state *at all*, and would carry none even if every reporter were perfectly sincere and striving for greater rigor. And it is **self-defeating**: made on the claimant's own authority, the claim reserves exactly the adjudicative standing that the practice it asserts forswears, so that issuing it instantiates the failure it denies. On this diagnosis, better reporters are **not** a remedy, because reporter quality was never the variable that failed in the first place. 

That the argument does not turn on anyone's insincerity matters, because the natural response to the first charge — exhort people to be more honest about their intellectual dishonesty — has been tried continuously for a century, and the second charge explains why it could not have worked.

§§3.2–3.3 establish the first half, uninformativeness. The second half, self-defeat, waits until §3.7, since stating it precisely requires machinery this section has yet to build. 

## 3.2 Self-attribution as cheap talk 

A signal carries information about a state of the world only if its probability differs across the states it is supposed to discriminate. If a signal is equally likely whether or not the state obtains, observing it tells you nothing you did not already know. 

Consider what a claim to intellectual honesty costs. It is available to anyone, at any time, for nothing. The scrupulously honest inquirer can say *I follow the evidence where it leads*; so can the inquirer whose every conclusion is fixed in advance. Both do say it, constantly (out loud or implicitly), and with equal conviction. (Or, perhaps it is ironically the dishonest who have even greater conviction on average.) Thus: 

$$
P(\text{claim} \, \mid \, \text{intellectually honest}) = P(\text{claim} \, \mid \, \text{not intellectually honest}) \approx 1 \, \Rightarrow \, LR = 1
$$

The likelihood ratio is one. The signal is what economists call cheap talk: an utterance with no cost differential across the states it purports to distinguish, and therefore with no discriminating power. The formal result is Crawford and Sobel's: where the interests of sender and receiver diverge, costless messages transmit only coarse information, and past a threshold of divergence they transmit none at all.[^cs] 

---

[^cs]: Crawford and Sobel (1982). Farrell and Rabin (1996) is a more accessible treatment. The biological literature on costly signaling reaches related conclusions by a different route, though the interpretation of the classic "handicap" results has been substantially revised — see Zollman, Bergstrom, and Huttegger (2013) on partially honest communication, which is closer to the graded picture I rely on than a cheap/costly dichotomy would be.

---

This is not a defect of any particular claimant. It is a property of the utterance type. 

A clarification is needed here, since the natural inference from what I've said is that a credible signal must be an expensive one, and that is not born out by the literature. What sustains informative signaling is not that the honest signaler pays a high price, but that **deviation would be costly**. Consider a company audit. A business with accurate books hands over records it was keeping anyway — near zero cost. A business cooking its books is the one that must pay the cost of fabricating a parallel set of internally consistent records or get caught. In this context, realized cost at an equilibrium of intellectual honesty may be near zero: the person who would have revised their view anyway pays nothing to be seen revising it. The cost falls on whoever would hold the signal without doing the thing it reports, and it falls on them at the point of deviation. I return to this in §5, where I constrain what an adequate check would have to look like; here it matters only as a caution against reading the diagnosis as a demand for costly display. It is not. 

## 3.3 Why a record of corrections does not repair it

The obvious reply is that some people do more than assert intellectual honesty. They keep a record. They publicly acknowledge their errors, and the acknowledgments accumulate into evidence of a disposition. Surely a demonstrated history of correction is not cheap talk. 

Let $n$ be the number of errors in a person's public discourse that others have identified, and $k$ the number they have publicly acknowledged. A corrections record is an implicit claim that $k/n$ is high — that when shown to be wrong, this person acknowledges as much. 

The difficulty is that **the claimant supplies both terms**. They decide which criticisms rose to the level of meriting a response, which is actually prior to $n$; and they decide which of those objections land, which *is* $n$, and they decide to acknowledge the error, $k$, thus easily keeping $k/n$ equal to $1$ (a 100% track record of updating in response to evidence) if they want, which is the whole idea. 

Importantly, and structurally, no one else is deciding what $n$ is, nor can they. **That's the whole disagreement that makes this ratio meaningless**. The person certifying their own intellectual honesty has no choice but to adjudicate whether an objection to something they've claimed has landed. If an observer disagrees and has a larger $n$ for the claimant, the additional errors structurally cannot enter into the claimant's own $n$ register without the claimant agreeing that they are in fact errors. Any criticism the claimant ignores or incorrectly dismisses simply cannot appear in the denominator. Self-monitoring is unavoidable. And crucially, it does not matter how sincere the claimant's belief is that the criticism fails. 

This holds with full force even when every acknowledged correction is genuine. Suppose a person's corrections are scrupulously accurate, generously worded, and promptly issued. The record is still uninformative about the ratio it is offered as evidence for, because the denominator was never determined by anyone but its author. A perfectly truthful numerator over a *structurally* unverifiable denominator yields no fraction at all. 

The point generalizes past any individual failing. The reason no one has ever produced a credible record of their own responsiveness is not that people are unwilling. It is that **no one has ever been in a position to do otherwise**. There has never existed a mechanism by which one person could check another person's denominator. The absence is not a shortfall of virtue; **it is a gap in the available instruments**.

Nor can the defect be engineered away from inside. Suppose the claimant, anticipating all of this, adopts criteria for what counts as a landed objection — drafted carefully, even in collaboration with a panel of experts, and however valid in principle. The criteria still have to be applied to particular cases, and the applier is still the claimant. Motivated interpretation operates on the application of criteria exactly as it operated on the raw judgments the criteria were meant to discipline. This merely relocates the discretion; it does not remove it. 

## 3.4 What an external check would have to detect

If self-report structurally cannot provide what it claims to provide, the question becomes what an external check of intellectual honesty would have to look for. I suggest three failure modes, each of which a competent check must be able to detect, either independently of the others or as a derivation from them. 

The first is **evasion**: declining scrutiny that is directed at one's own claims about objective reality. 

The second is **unresponsiveness**: engaging with evidence that bears against one's position and leaving one's confidence where it was. 

The third is **asymmetry**: demanding of others' claims a standard of rigor that one does not apply to structurally matched claims of one's own. 

The first two are independent, and it is worth establishing this carefully, since the temptation is to treat them as two descriptions of one vice. Consider an inquirer who appears at every challenge, engages at length, argues with energy and good humor, and never once revises a position. She avoids the first failure mode (evasion) and commits the second. I suspect this is the commonest sophisticated form. 

Consider next an inquirer who revises readily whenever evidence does happen to reach him, but who never voluntarily submits a claim for examination and/or never answers an invitation to open discussion on a topic he doesn't feel epistemically secure about. He avoids the second failure mode (unresponsiveness) but commits the first. 

Consider finally an inquirer who never evades, revises whenever the evidence obliges, but seeks to reserve her demands for rigor exclusively for positions she opposes. She avoids the first two failure modes, in other words, but has a tendency to commit the third (unwittingly, perhaps). Can she do so under sustained adversarial enforcement of the first two norms? Not if the first two checks are in fact satisfied. If she makes demands of others' claims that she does not meet herself, they are motivated to press the matched demand back on her and to challenge the standard outright; and since a standard of evidence is itself a claim, it is subject to the first two checks like any other. 

Thus, as long as a system applies the first two conditions recursively — by applying them to demands and standards as well as to first-order claims — it will detect the third failure mode without needing to enforce it separately. (The recursion is not free, and I do not assume it here: §5 derives it from the conditions an adequate check must meet, and the present claim is conditional on that derivation.) So the three are not three independent vices. They are the three ways the same thing (intellectual dishonesty) shows itself, and the third is the one a check gets for free once the first two are enforced at every level. 

## 3.5 Why each failure is categorical rather than a matter of degree

At this point a natural retreat presents itself. Everyone, it will be said, falls short on all three counts to some extent. Intellectual honesty is an ideal that no one fully attains, and what we are describing is a spectrum along which people are distributed. If so, the analysis yields a ranking rather than a verdict, and the interesting question becomes who ranks where. 

I will resist this, because each of the three failures has a structure that a spectrum interpretation obfuscates. In each case the behavior presupposes a rule, and in each case the rule cannot be defended. 

The argument has a common form: 

>A given behavior presupposes a rule $R$. $R$ is not coherently defensible. Acting on a presupposition whose defense is self-defeating is incompatible with practicing intellectual honesty. Therefore the behavior is incompatible with the practice. 

For example: to decline scrutiny of a claim persistently and without offering a defeasible reason presupposes that the claim is exempt from scrutiny of the kind appropriate to it. To hold a credence unmoved by evidence one has accepted presupposes that the jurisdiction of justification does not extend to oneself. To demand rigor of others that one does not apply to one's own matched claims presupposes that rigor is owed by others and not by oneself. None of these rules survives being stated, let alone universalized.

A second retreat is more instructive. Grant that the failures are categorical. An inquirer may still say: I hold nothing exempt — except one thing. On every other commitment I revise when the evidence demands it, readily and often. Surely a practice that reaches all but one of a person's claims is still the practice. 

The reply is perhaps the most crucial part of this paper: **an exemption is not a subtraction but a complete re-description**, and treating it as a subtraction mistakes what an exemption is. 

To hold a claim exempt from scrutiny is to hold that it need not answer to evidence, and something must have the standing to decide that. There are two possibilities. **The standing may not be the inquirer's.** She may hold that reasons of a general kind — about the sort of claim this is, or the sort of assessment it admits of — establish that it is not answerable in the way others are. But then those reasons are open to challenge, whatever established the exemption can withdraw it, and she has not exempted anything; she has produced a conclusion about how a claim should be assessed. **Or the standing may be her own.** If the exemption rests on nothing beyond her decision to exempt, that standing was never confined to the claim it was exercised on. It was available for all others, and remains available. So her many revisions were not instances of being bound by evidence, since she never was bound; they were instances of evidence pointing where she was in any case willing to go. **The exemption does not remove one item from the scope of the practice. It discloses the terms on which the whole of it was being conducted.** Only the second possibility is an exemption in the sense at issue, and one question distinguishes them wherever they are met: what could withdraw it, and by what procedure?[^exempt]

---

[^exempt]: The first possibility can be put more strongly. On the strong reading of "exempt" — outside rational jurisdiction altogether — the conjunction *this claim is exempt, and the reason for its exemption is not* is contradictory: if the ground can be rationally overturned, a rational path to the claim's exposure exists, and a claim with such a path is not outside rational jurisdiction. On the weak reading the conjunction is consistent, but what it describes is a revocable permit, which is the first possibility and no exemption at all.

---

The structure is the one familiar from the rule of law, which admits both kinds of exemption. Diplomatic immunity exempts a class of persons from ordinary process without the legal order ceasing to be one: the exemption is granted by law, its extent is defined in law, and it is revocable by the same process that could repeal any other law. The locus of sovereignty has not moved. What destroys a legal order is the exemption no law granted and no law can revoke, whose extent is set by the person holding it. Every judgment the order issues is then issued under an authority that could have suspended it, and everyone else's compliance stands revealed as subject to the whim of the one person exempt from it. **Such an exemption does not subtract a millionth from the rule of law; it relocates sovereignty.** Everyone else's law-abiding conduct is unchanged, but it has stopped being the relevant fact about the system. 

It bears pointing out that the exempting rule cannot be confined to the claim it was adopted for. Either it cites some feature of that claim — that it is sacred, or foundational, or not amenable to ordinary methods — in which case it licenses the identical exemption for every claim sharing that feature, including those held by people whose exemptions the inquirer rejects. Or it cites no feature, in which case the rule is that she may exempt whatever she chooses, which is a reservation of authority — a reservation that anyone else could make for themselves — rather than a rule. 

One qualification, and it bears on what exactly is being charged. Nothing here faults anyone for holding a belief they cannot defend. A person who holds such a belief, acknowledges it is unfalsifiable, and refrains from asserting it as true has made no demand on anyone, and there is no failure of intellectual honesty in that. What produces the failure is a specific conjunction: **asserting a claim as true**, which is to imply that anyone practicing intellectual honesty should come to believe it, **while holding the claim exempt from rational scrutiny**. Truth claims can thus differ from beliefs even where they overlap, and the same conjunction reappears as the corollary in §6. 

This is why the quantitative reading of intellectual honesty is at once so natural and so misleading. On most of their substantive commitments most people probably do revise when the evidence requires it, and if intellectual honesty were a quantity, high scores would be the norm. But a small number of exemptions is enough to produce post-truth politics and two lucid, good-natured people living in divergent realities. 

What §4 has occasion to note is that the small number of exemptions are hardly distributed at random; they fall on precisely those claims that no external process adjudicates — the claims most apt to become bound up with identity. These are the claims over which public disagreement is most consequential and least tractable. 

## 3.6 The terminal premise

The three-part schema in §3.4 rests on a premise that underwrites all three manifestations of intellectual dishonesty (evasion, unresponsiveness and asymmetry), and it deserves separate treatment, since a reader who rejects it rejects the whole argument. 

>**(P)** Acting on a presupposition whose defense is self-defeating is incompatible with practicing intellectual honesty. 

Two clarifications before the defense. First, *whose defense is self-defeating* is not *for which no proof exists*. Our most basic inferential commitments, induction chief among them, admit of no non-circular defense, and if (P) targeted those it would follow that no one has ever been intellectually honest. But a rule-circular defense and a self-defeating one are different defects. The first fails to persuade someone who does not already accept the rule; the second destroys the conclusion in the act of offering it. (P) targets only the second.

Second, *acting on*. Merely harboring unexamined presuppositions is the human condition and no one's failing. (P) engages when the presupposition is operative in the formation or maintenance of a belief *and* its indefensibility has been surfaced. This keeps (P) from requiring omniscience about one's own commitments. 

(P) can be reached by two independent routes, and the independence is the point: a premise derivable more than one way is differently situated from one stipulated once. 

**By retortion**. Suppose someone denies (P). Ask whether they are defending that denial or merely acting on it. If defending it, they have treated coherent defensibility as the standard their own claims must meet, and owe an account of why it applies here and not to the presuppositions (P) concerns. If not, they have announced rather than argued, and the position has not entered the discussion. Either they supply the grounds and concede the standard, or they exit. 

**By the ontology of the practice**. Intellectual honesty, on the account I take to be correct, is not a property one possesses but a practice one engages in — the practice of self-consistently holding one's truth claims answerable to reasons. If that is what the practice consists in, then acting on a presupposition that is by construction unanswerable to reasons is not a forbidden instance of the practice. It is simply not the practice. On this route (P) is not a rule constraining the activity from the outside; it is a consequence of what the activity is. 

It may be objected that (P) is a definition smuggled in as a premise. The charge, properly understood, is one of arbitrariness — that the premise was selected because it yields the wanted result, and that a different selection was equally available. It was not. (P) constrains what intellectual honesty can be without offering an account of what it is, and the constraint is not one I was free to choose: it is reachable by the routes above, none of which presupposes it, and its contrary cannot be coherently asserted, since to assert it is either to submit the assertion to the standard it denies or to decline to argue at all. A premise one is not free to reject is the opposite of a stipulation. 

And it may be objected that someone might act on an indefensible presupposition and arrive at the truth. So they might; **the practice is constituted by how beliefs are held rather than by whether they happen to be correct**. Arriving at truth by a route one cannot defend is not an instance of practicing intellectual honesty. It is merely happening to be right. 

Each of §3.4's failure modes is underwritten by (P), since §3.5 showed each to rest on a rule that cannot be defended. Unresponsiveness is only an exemption in disguise: to subject a claim to scrutiny and then refuse to adjust one's credence to the outcome is not to have subjected it at all. 

## 3.7 What follows

Intellectual honesty is not merely difficult to verify. Self-report cannot in principle verify it (§3.2), and a corrections record cannot repair this, since the claimant supplies both of its terms (§3.3). What is more, self-report presupposes a rule that cannot be defended: if I may self-certify my intellectual honesty, so may everyone, and the attribution then tracks nothing. §6 leans on this rule's failure; it is established here. 

And there is a stronger way to put the whole of it. **A self-certified claim to intellectual honesty is a counterexample to itself.** To claim the practice is to claim that one holds one's claims answerable *to reasons*. To self-certify is to hold exactly one claim — the claim about one's own conduct — structurally answerable *to no one but oneself*, since the claimant retains the authority to decide when an objection to it or to an argument for any claim they make has landed. 

Run §3.5's analysis on that reservation. The self-certified claim presupposes a rule: *my own verdict on whether an objection has landed settles the matter of my epistemic conduct.* That is an exemption, and of the second kind, because the standing behind it is nobody's but the claimant's — nothing beyond their own decision holds the exemption in place, and nothing but their own decision could withdraw it. The rule cannot be defended without self-defeat. Defend it, and the defense is an argument offered for outside assessment — which concedes that the claimant's verdicts answer to something beyond the claimant after all, and the reservation collapses. Decline to defend it, and the claim to intellectual honesty stands as a demand for assent issued by someone who exempts themselves from the one thing that could earn assent; it has been announced, not argued. 

And the exemption re-describes everything else, exactly as §3.5 says: a standing that settles when objections land on *this* claim was equally available for every other claim the claimant holds, so the record of concessions and revisions offered as evidence of answerability was conducted, all along, on terms the claimant could suspend at will. Nor is there a moderate version of any of this. One either retains final authority over the verdicts on one's own conduct or one does not; there is no partial reservation, which is why the failure is categorical rather than a matter of degree. 

This is a fact about the structure of the claim, whoever makes it and however skilled they are; it attaches to the assertion, not to the person, and §6.5 takes up why no claimant has been at fault for it. Note also what it does not touch: private practice. A person who reflects carefully and asserts nothing has claimed nothing — it is self-certification that self-defeats, not practice. 

What is required then is an external check capable of detecting at least the three distinct failure modes (evasion, unresponsiveness, asymmetry), each of which involves not a shortfall in degree but the adoption of a rule that cannot be defended. And the self-defeating result adds a constraint on the check itself. What defeated self-certification was not who made the claim but who held the verdict on it; a check that merely hands that verdict to some other single party puts the administrator in the claimant's position with respect to their own rulings. §5 derives this constraint properly; §4 will find it violated in a patterned way. 

The natural reply is that we are not without such checks. Peer review, adversarial legal procedure, and forecasting tournaments all subject claims to external examination and impose costs for failure. If any of these supplies what §3 has shown self-report cannot, the problem is solved and the argument ends here. §4 examines them. 

# 4. Whether we already have what is needed

## 4.1 The objection to the gap 

Before asking what an adequate check of intellectual honesty would look like, I should answer an objection that any reader of the preceding section might well have formed: that we possess several such checks, that they are among the most successful epistemic and trust-building institutions our civilization has produced, and that the difficulty I have described was solved long ago. 

The objection deserves to be represented at full strength. Peer review subjects claims to hostile scrutiny by experts who have both the competence to find fault and, frequently, the motive. Adversarial legal procedure is built on the premise that truth is best approached through opposed advocacy under rules that neither side administers. Forecasting tournaments do something neither of the others attempts: they score people against reality itself, and the score is a number that (usually) no one can argue with. If any of these supplies what §3 showed self-report cannot — i.e., warranted attributions of intellectual honesty — then what I have identified is a problem about individuals speaking loosely, not a problem about our epistemic institutions, and this paper can stop here. 

I want to be clear about what follows. My claim is not that these institutions fail. Each is excellent or at least adequate at what it was built for, and each supplies a genuine external adversarial check on *something*. My claim is that not one of them was built for this — for making warranted attributions of intellectual honesty. And more usefully, that they fall short in a patterned way. For the off-label purpose of enabling warranted attributions of intellectual honesty, each fails on a different property, and the properties they fail on turn out not to be independent of one another. It is that pattern, rather than any individual verdict, that I want the reader to take from this section. 

## 4.2 Peer review

Peer review supplies a real external check by parties with a real interest in finding fault. On the first two things one would want from such a check — that it come from outside, and that whoever administers it be motivated to find something — it performs about as well as any institution we have.[^lee]

---

[^lee]: And the familiar charge that it is pervasively biased is weaker than its reputation: Lee, Sugimoto, Zhang, and Cronin (2013), reviewing the empirical literature, find the evidence for many hypothesized forms of bias to be limited. Nothing in this section requires peer review to perform badly, and I do not claim it does.

---

The difficulty is elsewhere, and it is a difference of target rather than of quality.[^hb] Peer review asks whether *this* paper survives scrutiny. It never asks whether *you* revised. 

---

[^hb]: The observation that follows about administration is not mine alone. Heesen and Bright (2021), arguing on quite different grounds — epistemic-consequentialist, and toward the abolition of prepublication review — rest much of their case on the arbitrariness of concentrating judgment in an editor and a handful of reviewers. Worth noticing for present purposes: their proposed replacement, post-publication community evaluation, would still certify *papers*. The reform debate concerns how claims should be checked; no party to it proposes checking anyone's conduct. That is not a defect; it is the institution doing precisely what it was designed to do. But it has a consequence for the question at hand. A scientist may say, with complete honesty and with an institution to back her, *"my papers are checked."* She cannot say *"I am checked"* — and it is the second of these that a claim to intellectual honesty asserts.

---

Alongside that difference of target sit three further limitations, and I want to draw attention to the relation between them rather than to the items themselves. Peer review is administered: editors determine what is sent for review, what is published, and whether an appeal is entertained. Given that, the journal's remit reserves authority in an editorial board, which makes certain claims structurally unactionable. There is no procedure by which a winning argument against peer review obligates peer review to yield to some alternative, and there is no inherent cost to ignoring one. And the relation runs in one direction only: a reviewer's demands of a paper are not themselves subject to symmetric review. An appeal to the editorial board is itself an asymmetric relation. Finally, certain claims can be unilaterally ruled inadmissible as outside of the journal's scope. 

I have listed those three as though they were separate findings. They are not. Each is an *exemption* — a region placed outside the check, a set of mechanics placed outside the check, a party placed outside the check. All three trace to the fact that someone is administering the check. I will leave the observation at that here; §5 arrives at the same point from a different direction, and I would rather the reader recognize it there than be told it twice. 

And concerning intellectual honesty, there is also nothing to read. A publication record is not a determinate status about anyone's holistic epistemic conduct. It is a count of surviving papers, and it is compatible with a great many different underlying practices. Here again the shortfall is not a flaw but an absence of design intent: nothing in the institution was ever meant to produce such a status.[^wiki]

---

[^wiki]: Wikipedia belongs to the same family and falls short for the same reason. It is adversarial, unusually close to arbiter-free, and maintains a genuine public record of disputes — but what it certifies is *article content*, not contributors. The difference of target again.

---
## 4.3 Adversarial legal procedure

Adversarial legal procedure is the most developed adversarial machinery in existence: opposed advocacy, rules of evidence, and a determinate verdict about what is most warranted by the evidence. That last one supplies something peer review does not — a determinate, readable outcome. A judgment is not a matter of interpretation. 

But the institution is organized around allocating liability under procedural constraint, not around accuracy as such, and certainly not around anyone's revision or responsiveness to evidence. **A trial produces a verdict; it does not produce a series of provisional, increasingly well-supported, evidence-tracking updates.** And the losing advocate does not revise her position; she appeals. Nowhere in the institution is there a measure of whether any participant moved in response to what was shown to them. 

A second difficulty is more instructive. Advocates argue positions they need not hold. It would be easy, and wrong, to call this a problem of insincerity: the assignment is public and no one is deceived. The problem is structural. **There is no declared position of the advocate's own for a status to be about.** Whatever a trial measures about counsel, it is her skill rather than her honesty — not because she is dishonest, but because her own view never entered the record. This matters in §5: a determinate status about a person's epistemic conduct presupposes that the person's own position is in the record, and no requirement about anyone's inner states is needed to see it. 

The institution is also administered — judge and jury — and its scope limits (justiciability, standing, jurisdiction) are set by the same authority, constrained by a procedure that the authority itself administers. One concession, because it complicates the picture usefully: procedural rules in law *are* adjudicable within the system. Courts rule on the rules, and precedent can be overturned. That is partial self-correction, arguably more than peer review manages, and it shows that these properties come apart — an institution can possess some and lack others, which is what makes them independent requirements rather than one package under different descriptions. 

## 4.4 Forecasting tournaments

Forecasting tournaments are the closest existing case, and I want to say so plainly rather than treat them as a third example.[^tet] The result is objectively readable — a number requiring no interpretation. It is graded. It is responsive: the score moves when you were wrong, and nothing you do can stop it moving. And the check is external in an unusually strong sense, since reality supplies the verdict. 

---

[^tet]: Tetlock, Mellers, Rohrbaugh, and Chen (2014) for the tournaments and their scoring. 

---

It is tempting to conclude that there is no arbiter here at all. That is not true, and the truth is more useful. Consider a question asking whether a central bank's policy rate will fall below three per cent by some date, with resolution criteria published in advance: where the bank adopts a target *range*, the rate is to be read from its midpoint. Several hundred people forecast. The bank then adopts a range whose midpoint falls exactly on the threshold. Someone has to rule on what the question was asking, and an administrator does. 

So arbitration has not been eliminated. What has changed is *when* it occurs and *whether it can be aimed*. When the criteria were written, their author did not know a range would be adopted, did not know the midpoint would land on the threshold, and did not know which forecasters would exist or which side any would take. The discretion was exercised behind a veil, and a biased author could not have made the bias effective, because the information needed to aim it did not yet exist. **The untargetability is structural rather than moral.** Compare the editor deciding whether to send a paper out for review, who knows the claim, the author, the institution, and often the likely reviewers. The same act of discretion is aimable in one case and not the other. 

The administrator nonetheless remains present: ruling on ambiguous resolutions, choosing the resolution convention, and deciding which questions exist at all. The last bears on the limitation forecasting is usually said to have, **scope**. A tournament reaches only claims that resolve within some horizon; theological, moral, historical, health-related, economic causal claims and most political claims lie outside it, because forecasting reality cannot score a claim it never adjudicates. That is a limit of the world, and it falls exactly where the need is greatest, since the claims on which straightforwardly readable reality is silent are the ones that become bound up with identity. But the set of questions actually asked is chosen by whoever authors them, which is scope bounded by authority in the ordinary way. Both limits are real; only the first is unavoidable. 

This forces a concession: adversarial *persons* are not the only possible enforcers; where reality renders verdicts, scoring does the same work. §5's second condition has to be stated so as to admit this. 

## 4.5 The pattern

|                                                                       | Peer review                                                 | Adversarial law                                    | Forecasting                            |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| External check                                                        | ✓                                                           | ✓                                                  | ✓                                      |
| Adversarial (in the spirit of adversarial collaboration or otherwise) | ✓                                                           | ✓                                                  | *reality substitutes*                  |
| Readable record of the check                                          | only partial (*reviews confidential; rejections invisible*) | ✓ (*public judgment, with reasons*)                | ✓ (*public score*)                     |
| Discretion over identified parties                                    | heaviest; ex post; aimable                                  | heavy; procedurally constrained, however           | lightest; **ex ante; unaimable**       |
| Mechanics contestable from within                                     | ✗                                                           | partial *(precedent)*                              | ✗ *(conventions, question selection)*  |
| Reach                                                                 | all falsifiable scholarly claims                            | justiciable claims                                 | near-term, cleanly resolvable claims   |
| Boundary authored and aimable                                         | yes *(remit, desk rejection)*                               | yes, rule-constrained *(justiciability, standing)* | partly; ex ante *(question selection)* |
| **Status about the person**                                           | ✗                                                           | ✗                                                  | *calibration, not honesty*             |

Three things emerge from the table, and the first two run in opposite directions. 

First, the exemption-type shortfalls do not merely cluster; they *scale*. Where an authority administers a check, three things appear: the edge of the check (the boundary line between what the institution will and won't examine) is set by that authority and can be aimed at particular claims, the check's own mechanics are placed outside it, and it runs in one direction only. Each appears in proportion to how much discretion the authority exercises over identified parties and claims. Peer review, where discretion is heaviest and most aimable, shows all three in full; adversarial procedure, whose rules the courts can revisit, shows partial self-correction; forecasting, where discretion is lightest and exercised before anyone can be identified, shows all three attenuated (nature authors the edge of the check — not artificially imposed discretion). The amount of each shortfall tracks the amount of discretion. A dose-response relationship of that kind is better evidence than co-occurrence alone, since it is what one would expect only if discretion were the cause. The mechanism is that each shortfall is an *exemption*, and an exemption has to be granted by someone with the standing to grant it.[^platforms] 

---

[^platforms]: Structured argumentation platforms — Kialo and the argument-mapping tradition — belong here as further cases, not near misses. They keep a persistent inspectable record of an exchange, but they are administered by their operators and represent the structure of a debate rather than any status about a participant. See Klein and Iandoli (2008) and Bench-Capon and Dunne (2007). 

---

Second, *reach* runs the other way, and this is the finding I would ask the reader to hold onto. Peer review can be brought to bear on any falsifiable scholarly claim, which is nearly the whole of what can be objectively known about nature, the human sphere included. Adversarial law reaches only justiciable claims. Forecasting reaches only claims that resolve soon and cleanly, which excludes moral, theological, historical, and most scholarly and political claims on which reality's verdict is complex, slow, absent or contested. So the institution that is nearly free of an arbiter has the narrowest reach, and the one with the widest reach has the heaviest arbiter. That is not a coincidence but a trade. Peer review buys its reach with human adjudicators, and human adjudicators are where discretion lives. Forecasting buys its near arbiter-freedom by letting reality adjudicate, and reality adjudicates only where it is quick and unambiguous. No existing institution has both wide reach and freedom from an arbiter, and a warranted attribution of intellectual honesty needs both, since there is no domain-limited intellectual honesty to attribute (§3.5). What would be required is adversarial enforcement from humans, for reach, without a human arbiter. Nothing we have is built that way, and §5 will say what would have to be true of something that was. 

Third, whatever each institution reads out, it is not about the person. Peer review yields a status about **papers**, and even that only in outline, since the reviews are confidential and the rejections invisible, so that no one's $k/n$ of §3.3 could easily be computed from a publication record. Adversarial procedure yields a status about **cases**. Forecasting yields one about **calibration** — a real property of a person, but not the one at issue, since a well-calibrated forecaster may be entirely closed on everything her tournament does not score. Not one of these institutions was built to answer *is this person practicing intellectual honesty?* — perhaps a large part of why the answer has never been available. 

In summary, §4's findings suggest that an adequate check would need to be external, adversarial (both of which are satisfied by the institutions surveyed), a readable record about the participant's epistemic conduct, be arbiter-free, unbounded in epistemic scope (wide in reach), not exempt the check's own mechanics, and recursively apply to the checker's own demands and assumptions, not only to the claims checked.

If none of these institutions supplies what is needed for the purpose of making warranted attributions of intellectual honesty, the question is what would. A specification assembled by patching their gaps would inherit their assumptions, so §5 derives its requirements from the diagnosis in §3 instead, and arrives by an independent route at a set of conditions containing those listed above. 

# 5. What an adequate check would have to be

## 5.1 The question, and how I propose to answer it

The two preceding sections were negative. Self-report cannot supply evidence of intellectual honesty, and the institutions we have supply real checks that fail in a patterned way: wherever an authority administers the check, the edge of the check is authored by that authority, the check's own mechanics are exempt, and it runs one way, each in proportion to the discretion exercised; reach and arbiter-freedom trade against each other, so that none has both; and whatever each reads out is a status about papers, cases, or calibration, never about a person's epistemic conduct. 

What would an adequate check look like? I want to answer in a particular way and be explicit about the method, because the alternative is a list of desirable properties assembled by intuition, and such lists are worth little. Each condition below is derived from the **diagnosis**, and each by the same move. §3 established that self-report fails because the signal is equally probable whether or not the state obtains. A remedy can fail the same way one level up: the check can be structured so that *it too* passes regardless of the state. **Each condition below thus forecloses one such relocation.** I have found four: the check may be **self-supplied**; the checker may be **indifferent** to what it finds; the record may be **too interpretable** or determinate about something other than the participant; or an **authority** may decide outcomes. A fifth relocation would require a fifth condition, and I would want to know of such a relocation. 

That gives the reader a test, and I ask that it be applied to every condition below: **does dropping this condition let the §3 problem back in?** If not, the condition does not belong here, whatever else recommends it. 

## 5.2 What makes a check informative

Two general points first. The first was made in §3.2 and needs only recalling: what sustains an informative signal is not that honest participants pay a high price but that **deviation** is costly. **The conditions below therefore describe a structure nearly free to those already doing the thing and expensive only to defect from**. A check that imposed heavy costs on honest participants would be the wrong shape; it would measure endurance rather than honesty. 

The second marks the limit of the signaling frame, because the conditions do not describe a signal in Spence's sense, and a reader who carries that picture into 5.3 will misread them. In the Spence signaling model the state is private and permanently unobservable, and a separate visible act licenses an inference across the gap by its *cost*, not its content. What follows differs in three ways. The information lies in **content**: what a participant said when a particular objection was put to them, where a different response at identical cost changes the inference entirely. The examination is **generated adversarially**: a counterparty chooses which claims to press, and is naturally motivated to press where the participant seems likeliest to refuse to practice intellectual honesty. And the constraint on anyone who would hold the status without doing the thing is not price but **feasibility**: some responses are unavailable for purchase at any cost, since producing one would require having done the work it reports — i.e., there is no way to buy a record showing that you conceded an objection (or adequately engaged and answered it) other than by conceding it (or adequately answering it). Selectively conceding doesn't get around it, and inner insincerity is out of scope. The barrier to faking is that faking well *is indistinguishable from doing the thing*. That places the structure nearer to the literature on verification than to costly signaling,[^verif] and it means no separating equilibrium across a population is needed; each record is about one participant and stands on its own. 

---

[^verif]: Townsend (1979) on costly state verification; Green and Laffont (1986) on partially verifiable information. I draw the analogy only to locate the structure, and claim no result in either literature. 

---

One consequence is worth stating, because it discharges the promise made to the dispositional reader in §1. On the practice account, the record is not evidence for a hidden state; **the conduct it records is what the practice consists in**. On a dispositional account, the same record is evidence from which a trait may be inferred, as conduct is ordinarily evidence of character. **The conditions in 5.3 are identical either way.** None requires anything of anyone's inner states, and where an inference to a disposition is drawn, it is the reader who draws it and nothing in the system does. The disagreement is real and I have not settled it. It does not need settling for the argument to go through. 

## 5.3 Four conditions

**(a) The check must be external** — not supplied by the person whose intellectual honesty is at issue. This follows directly from §3. The constitutive step of the practice is recognizing when an objection has landed, by evaluating arguments only on their merit. That is the step most exposed to motivated reasoning. A person grading their own performance on it is performing a version of the practice where its hardest component is self-assessed by the party with the largest stake in the result. 

**(b) The check must be adversarial** — the checker must have some interest in finding fault. An external check by someone indifferent fails for §3's reason exactly: if the checker has no stake, the check passes whether or not the person is practicing intellectual honesty, so observing that it passed tells us nothing. 

It will be objected that (b) makes (a) redundant. They come apart, narrowly. Someone playing genuine devil's advocate against her own position has something like the adversarial interest while failing externality, and she fails for the §3 reason, since it is still she who decides whether her own objection landed. I should also concede the exception §4.4 earned: adversarial *persons* are not the only enforcers, since scored outcomes do the same work wherever reality renders a verdict. But persons are needed precisely where reality's verdict is complex, slow, absent, or itself contested, which is where the claims in question for the sake of assessing intellectual honesty overwhelmingly live, and not by coincidence. 

**(c) The record must be objectively readable** — it must exist, and it must yield a determinate status **about the participant's own epistemic conduct**, without interpretive discretion. Two things are asked for. A record whose meaning is contested is not yet a check: a transcript is inspectable, but whether someone conceded (or adequately engaged) in it is interpretable, and a status available either way carries no more information than the self-attribution it was meant to replace. This is the third appearance of the same failure. And a record can be perfectly determinate and still be about the wrong thing, as §4 found three times. Note what follows: **you cannot have a determinate status about someone's epistemic conduct on a claim unless their own position on that claim is in the record.** This is why assigned-side advocacy cannot serve, however skilled, and no appeal to the advocate's sincerity is required or wanted. 

**(d) There must be no arbiter** — no party with discretion over *identified* claims or participants; **the check must have a terminating replacement, and the rules standing in the arbiter's place must themselves be subject to the check.** Suppose the check is administered by an authority who determines whether objections have landed. That determination is itself an attribution of intellectual honesty; by (a) it requires an external check to be warranted; and if nothing checks the arbiter, their verdict is self-certified, which is the original problem relocated one level up and dressed in institutional clothing. 

I say '*identified* claims' deliberately, because without it the condition is unsatisfiable: every check is constituted by someone, and rule-setting cannot be eliminated, only disciplined. The discipline has two parts. Rules must be authored where they cannot be aimed — before the parties and claims they govern exist, which is the position the question-author in a tournament already occupies (§4.4). And they must remain challengeable afterward, with a winning argument obligating change, since a rule exempt from the system it governs is an arbiter in written form. The rule-author, unlike the arbiter, makes no attribution about anyone; at authoring time there is no one to make one about. The regress bites where discretion touches identified persons, and only there. E.g., an arbiter rules that Smith's objection didn't land. That ruling is an attribution about Smith's epistemic conduct. But by (a), an attribution needs an external check to be warranted. So now the arbiter's ruling needs a checker. But so does that checker's ruling, and so on. The regress doesn't stop. No such regress bites properly disciplined rule-setting. 

An alternative to removing the arbiter entirely may come to mind: equal and independent, mutual arbiters. One tier of arbiters is checked by a higher-tier arbiter, while a quorum of lower-tier arbiters can itself check the higher tier, or some such principle. This, however, merely replaces the simplicity of the single-arbiter case with a more complex one reintroducing its own arbitrariness. The point isn't checkability alone — it's the removal of authority's discretion itself. As long as the process hinges on authority applying discretion, the same underlying disease remains: arbitrariness and authority **rather than the parties' own concessions while visibly trying to find sustainable defenses**.  

It does not follow that arbiter-removal alone will suffice, however. Without an arbiter the regress runs the other way, indefinitely, and an unterminated regress yields no warrant either. So the condition asks three things at once: **no discretion over the identified claims, no rule outside the check, and disputes must terminate** — between the parties, at principles neither can coherently reject (i.e., **at a retortional layer**), with evidential weight propagating from resolved sub-questions to a determinate result on the original one. 

## 5.4 Three further properties are consequences, not requirements

Four conditions is already a great deal to demand, and a reader would be right to suspect a longer list of padding. Three properties one might expect as conditions are not in fact independent conditions. They follow from (d), by one principle: 

> **Each is an exemption. An arbiter-free system cannot grant exemptions, because exemption requires an authority empowered to grant it.** 

**Scope cannot be bounded.** A standing epistemic domain restriction could, like any rule, be authored before parties and claims exist; (d)'s first clause does not forbid that. What (d)'s second clause demands is that it then survive as a claim: challengeable by any participant, sustained by argument or not at all. And no such boundary can win that argument, because a bounded system yields a bounded status, and a bounded status cannot underwrite the claim at issue (that an individual practices intellectual honesty). As §3.5 argued and §6 relies on, there is no domain-limited intellectual honesty to attribute. What is excluded is not an authored boundary but an *exempt* one. Natural narrowness, of the kind that confines forecasting to what reality scores, is a different matter: it violates nothing in (d), since no one set it, and it is answered not here but by (b)'s concession that human adversaries are needed wherever reality is silent. This is the observation §4 reached from the other direction, without any of the present apparatus: the authored boundary scales with discretion, and reach trades against arbiter-freedom. The two routes are independent, and they meet here. 

**The mechanics cannot be exempt.** A designer who exempted the mechanics would be an arbiter for that class of claims, contradicting (d) — and this consequence is already visible inside (d)'s own statement. That is not redundancy but a relation worth pausing on. Self-correction does not merely follow from (d); it is what makes (d) *satisfiable*, since a system without it has residual rule-authoring accountable to no one. (d) names a constraint; self-correction names the mechanism that makes the constraint satisfiable. 

**The check must apply at every level.** Exempting a level requires someone empowered to exempt it, and there is no such person. Like the last, this also runs the other way: recursive application is part of how residual rule-authority is kept inside the check rather than above it. 

Two properties are *produced* rather than required. **Comprehensiveness is self-constructing**: participants may try to submit only safe epistemic positions, but an epistemic opponent targets what they take you to be most likely wrong about, **which is disproportionately your unexamined and protected material**. Adversarial selection is biased toward the unexamined; self-selection is biased away from it. And **symmetry is derived rather than enforced**, as §3.4 anticipated: a demand for rigor is itself a claim, and applied to the demand, (a) and (b) catch it. The matched-pair objection should ideally be expressible without asserting anything about a given person's hypocrisy, and it can be: it pairs a standards-shaped claim the agent accepts with a specific claim she also accepts that fails that standard. Both referents are propositions — neither labels the person. This requires that implicit standards be forced explicit when challenged, itself a consequence of the recursion. 

So the specification is shorter than it appears: **four conditions and three consequences**, all three flowing from one condition, though not symmetrically. Unbounded scope is a pure consequence. Self-correction and recursive application are consequences that double as enablers; (d) cannot be satisfied without them. That (d) carries so much is worth noticing, and I return to it in 5.7. 

## 5.5 Plurality, and why there can be only one ledger

The conditions do not select an implementation; they are silent on interface, governance, funding, and scale. They do not, however, permit an unresolved plurality of *verdicts*. Suppose two conforming systems — two systems that each meet the four core conditions — returned different statuses for the same claim. A reader must then choose which to credit, and that choice is adjudicated by neither. **This reinstates the arbiter**, distributed across readers rather than seated in an institution. **Further, a shoppable status is not a determinate status, because shopping is an act of unadjudicated authority.** What would've been a fulfillment of the four conditions in a single system is now a failure of two of them — (c) and (d) — in two such systems existing concurrently.

What must happen in such a situation? The divergence between the two verdicts is itself an unavoidable claim: that the other system's verdict, or the rule that produced it, is wrong. Unbounded scope and recursive application (5.4) require each system to admit that claim, and (d) requires each to be bound by its outcome. But binding adjudication presupposes a procedure both parties accept, and there are only three candidates: A's, B's, or a third that both accept. The first two subordinate one system to the other; the third is a single procedure. So there is no such thing as two conforming systems adjudicating their divergence at arm's length. Either they share one procedure, or at least one of them has exempted its own mechanics from the check, which is the one thing (d) forbids, and has done so on nobody's authority but its own. It is self-certifying its verdicts. 

Nor may the two persist as a legible deep disagreement about rules, in the way that two participants may persist in one about a first-order claim (§7.3). The termination clause of (d) applies to rules, not to participants' claims. A rule must terminate at principles no party can coherently reject, because a rule that a coherent party rejects and that is held in place anyway is held in place by fiat, and fiat is an arbiter. A participant may hold a contested first-order position without failing the practice; a system may not hold a contested rule without failing (d). 

The same argument reaches the record. Suppose two systems shared a procedure but kept separate records, so that a challenge to a participant's claim in one did not appear in the other. Then the participant chooses where to be seen, and §3.3's denominator has been handed back to the claimant. So the record must be one, and the dialogue over it fully connected: any claim challengeable by anyone, with every challenge landing in the one record. **Two independent conforming systems must therefore merge, in procedure and in record, or at least one of them is not conforming, and the record shows which. The party that insists on merging is the only party insisting on satisfying the conditions.** 

This is a uniqueness result, and I should say what it does and does not claim. It does not claim that there can be only one interface, one operator, or one source of funding; the first sentence of this subsection stands, and a plurality of front-ends over one substrate is exactly what the conditions permit. It claims that there can be only one ledger, and that no party owns it in the discretionary sense, since its mechanics are contestable by anyone and its statuses are decided by no one. A monopoly is an authority; this is the absence of one. What the argument leaves open is not whether multiple such systems will by chance emerge or whether their merger is achievable but whether anyone builds a conforming system at all, and how many people use it, which is §7.3's question about power (as opposed to validity).

>Nothing here is a norm of belief revision in the conciliationist sense (Christensen 2007): the conditions specify what makes an *attribution* warranted and prescribe no deference to any reader. (why is this here??)

---

The specification is falsifiable, and I state the falsifier plainly: exhibit either a system satisfying these conditions whose attributions are nonetheless unwarranted, or a warranted attribution of intellectual honesty produced without one. 

## 5.6 Representation

The conditions leave open what the status of a truth claim in the record should look like. It could be a binary verdict, *accepted* or *rejected*, or it could be a number between 0 and 1 expressing how much a claim is favored by evidence (e.g., over and against its catchall). The question deserves a paragraph because the simpler answer is more available than one might think. There is a well-developed formalism, the abstract argumentation frameworks of Dung (1995), in which arguments are nodes, attacks between arguments are edges, and a fixed rule computes from the attack structure alone which arguments stand and which fall. A record built that way is fully determinate, requires no arbiter, and uses no numbers and no prior probabilities. So condition (c), objective readability, does not by itself require anything continuous; a binary record can be perfectly readable. 

What a two-valued record cannot represent is **degree**, and §3's diagnosis needs degree in two places. The charge of unresponsiveness is that a person's confidence did not move at all after being presented with new evidence. That charge cannot even be stated unless confidence is something that moves by more or less, which a status of *stands* or *falls* is not. **And most real evidence supports a claim partially, raising or lowering its probability without settling it**. This is especially true under fallibilism. In a two-valued scheme partial support has nowhere to go: it either flips the verdict or is discarded. So the claim I would defend is narrower than "the record must be probabilistic." It is that (c), together with the requirement that evidence of varying strength be representable, requires a continuous substrate: statuses that are credences (degrees of confidence), or that behave like them. What the degree-of-confidence substrate buys is not determinacy, which the two-valued scheme already had, but *graded* determinacy: a determinate answer to how far a claim is favored by the evidence, and likewise a determinate answer to whether, and how much, a participant's confidence moved. 

## 5.7 A disclosure

A reader who has come this far may wonder whether these conditions were derived or reverse-engineered, since they are unusually specific and not many things satisfy them. **The author has built a system intended to satisfy these conditions.** It is described elsewhere and is not offered as evidence here. The conditions stand or fall on the derivations given, and I would ask the reader to apply the 5.1 test to each — including, especially, (d), which by the end of 5.4 is carrying more of the specification than any single condition comfortably should. 

# 6. A corollary

Everything so far has been diagnostic. Self-report cannot supply evidence of a person's epistemic conduct; the institutions we have supply it for other things; and an adequate check would have to satisfy four conditions with three further properties following from one of them. Nothing in that requires anyone to do anything. But a consequence follows, and I think it is the practical point of the paper. 

## 6.1 The result

> **Let $S$ be any system satisfying the conditions of §5, available to an agent $A$. Then $A$ cannot coherently defend the conjunction of (i) a public claim to practice intellectual honesty, and (ii) persistent refusal to participate in $S$ or any equivalent — unless $A$ supplies a defeasible reason for (ii) that does not itself presuppose that self-attribution suffices.** 

Three features of that statement are deliberate. It is a **conditional**, and its antecedent is contingent: if no such system exists, the result is vacuous. It concerns **defensibility**, not truth: someone may hold the conjunction — the claim is only that they cannot defend it. And its **exception is stated inside it** rather than left for someone who rejects it to discover. 

Note also what is absent. An alternative formulation of the statement might have read *"a public claim to practice intellectual honesty **holistically**."* The adverb is redundant, however, and removing it strengthens the result. §3.5 gave the reason: exempting even one substantive claim from scrutiny appropriate to its kind is not a small shortfall but a disclosure of the terms on which everything else was being conducted, and the exempting rule cannot be confined to the claim it was adopted for. **There is no such thing as domain-limited intellectual honesty.** This matters below, because it determines what the scientist who says *"my papers are peer-reviewed"* is actually saying. 

## 6.2 The argument

1. Warranted attribution of intellectual honesty requires an external adversarial check, because the practice's constitutive step — recognizing when an objection has landed — is the step most exposed to motivated reasoning and cannot be self-graded without undermining the practice (§3, §5.3). 
2. Where no system satisfying §5's conditions is available, solo practice counts as practice only insofar as it is parasitic on some real external one: standards set elsewhere, epistemic work eventually returned. 
3. Domain-local institutions supply a check on claims within a domain, not on the person (§4). 
4. Therefore a claim to intellectual honesty is warranted only where an unrestricted external check is available and used — every genuine such claim being unrestricted, per 6.1. 
5. Where a conforming $S$ exists and is available, refusing it while claiming intellectual honesty leaves the claim with no possible warrant. 
6. Asserting a practice while declining the available means of making it self-consistent, and offering no defeasible reason, presupposes the rule *self-attribution suffices*. 
7. That rule is not coherently defensible (§3.7). And universalized, it licenses the attribution for everyone regardless of conduct, destroying the distinction it exists to track. 
8. Acting on a presupposition whose defense is self-defeating is incompatible with practicing intellectual honesty (§3.6). 

The conjunction therefore cannot be coherently defended. Step 6 is where the argument's honesty lives. The presupposition is imputed **only when no reason is offered**. Supply a reason and step 6 fails, which is exactly right, and is what keeps the result narrow enough to be true. 

## 6.3 Which reasons survive

Two classes qualify under the *unless* clause, and both are contingent rather than principled. 

**Cold-start.** *There is nobody on $S$ in my field; participating would subject me to a form, not to enforcement.* This is coherent and it is the only surviving defense of the conjunction, and it **expires with adoption**. It is also weaker than it first appears, because entry is itself a costly signal independent of whether any counterparty materializes: it puts credences on a permanent record, and it arms a mechanism against the person entering. So the defense holds against the claim that *enforcement* is occurring, and not against the claim that a structurally distinct option was declined. 

**Domain coverage.** *My claims fall within a field already covered by external check.* Given 6.1, this does not narrow the claim to intellectual honesty; it **abandons** it, and substitutes a different and perfectly good claim about one's work. Anyone taking this route has conceded the argument rather than answered it, and I do not say that dismissively. It is the honest move, and it is available. 

Two further reasons are honest but do different work:

**Exposure** — participating puts one's beliefs on a record an employer, a state, or a congregation may use — is prudentially justified and morally blameless, but it defeats the *culpability* of the refusal rather than the *warrant gap* between the claim to intellectual honesty and demonstrable reality. The claim remains unwarranted, and the honest response is to stop making it publicly. 

**Design critique** — *your $S$ fails condition (d), for the following reason* — is participation at the level of the mechanics rather than refusal, and it argues for a different $S$ rather than for none. 

## 6.4 What the result does not say

It does **not** say that everyone must participate. It does **not** say that refusal alone is indefensible; refusal on its own is coherently defensible, and a great deal of it is reasonable. It does **not** say that non-participants have a *character flaw* of intellectual dishonesty (see §1). And it does **not** say that any particular system satisfies §5's conditions. The antecedent is contingent, and in the one case where I might be thought to have an interest, I have declined to assert it, clarifying instead that the *intent* is to satisfy the conditions (§5.7). Whether it *does* adequately do so remains to be seen. 

**Falsifier.** Exhibit an agent who publicly claims intellectual honesty, persistently refuses the available conforming system (see §5.5 for the argument against plurality of such systems), and supplies a defeasible reason for the refusal that is neither temporary, nor an abandonment of the claim, nor a presupposition that self-attribution suffices. 

## 6.5 The corollary

The result is vacuous while no conforming system exists. I take that to be a feature, and it yields what I think is the paper's most consequential sentence: 

> **Before any such system exists, no claimant can be faulted. After one exists, every claimant in a position to refuse such a system is newly on the hook — not because anyone's conduct changed, but because the option set did.** 

**This is a claim about history rather than about character**. It explains why self-certification has been universal *and blameless* for the entire span of human argument, and why that would cease to be true at a datable moment for specific people, determined by the availability of an instrument rather than by any change in what people are like. 

One refinement, for precision. The blamelessness has two grounds, and they can come apart. Nothing ever made the question salient of whether self-attributions of intellectual honesty are self-defeating — no institution, vocabulary, or practice put it in front of anyone — and even for someone to whom it became salient, no remedy for the missing warrant existed. But one remedy short of a conforming system is always available to a person who has seen the defect: **saying less**. So the position is three-staged. Before the defect is visible, no fault of any kind. After it is visible and before a conforming system exists, no fault attaches to lacking warrant, since warrant was unobtainable, but continued public assertion is now a choice, since silence was available; this is the same structure as the exposure case in 6.3. And after a conforming system exists, the result above. This is how the paper holds both that the claim was always self-defeating (§3.7) and that no claimant was at fault: blamelessness rests on salience, a specific conjunction, and remedy, not on the defect having been invisible by necessity. 

## 6.6 What this section is and is not for

I want to close by saying plainly what I am not doing, because the argument above has a shape that invites a reading I would reject. 

**It is not offered as pressure**, and specifically not as pressure to adopt the system I happen to have built. If the argument works, it works against a class of systems characterized by §5's conditions, of which mine is at most one member and possibly not a member at all. That is what the pilot is for, and I have declined to assert its adequacy as given in the meantime. 

**It is not offered to make anyone feel culpable.** §6.5 says explicitly that no one has been at fault, and I mean it in the strong sense: the people who have asserted their own intellectual honesty over the past several thousand years were doing the only thing available to them, and the fact that it carried no information is a fact about the available instruments and not about them. 

**It is offered as a deduction**, and nothing more. It follows, or it does not, from premises stated in §§3–5. If it does not follow, I would like to be shown where. That is a more valuable outcome than agreement. 

And people are of course free to reach their own conclusions about whether it holds, and free to decline it whether they judge it unsound or simply do not care either way. **That has to be true, or the argument would be refuting itself.** An argument that a person cannot coherently claim a virtue while refusing every check on it would be a strange thing to make by any means other than logical reasons. 

# 7. Discussion

## 7.1 What has and has not been established

It is worth separating these, because the argument is easy to inflate and I would rather do the deflating myself. 

**Established, as far as I can tell.** That self-report cannot supply evidence of a person's epistemic conduct, and would fail to even among sincere and accurate reporters. That a record of one's own corrections does not repair this, because the claimant supplies both of its terms. That a self-certified claim to intellectual honesty is a counterexample to itself, since it reserves the very adjudicative authority the practice forswears. That three distinct epistemic conduct failure modes would have to be detectable by any adequate check of intellectual honesty. That the institutions we possess supply real external checks whose exemption-type shortfalls scale with administered discretion, whose reach trades against their arbiter-freedom, and none of which yields a status about the person’s epistemic conduct. And that four conditions follow from the diagnosis, with three further properties following from or required by one of them. 

**Not established.** That any existing or proposed system satisfies those conditions, including mine. That a conforming system would be adopted, which is a different question again and one this paper does not raise. And that the specification is complete: I have argued that each condition is necessary for checking intellectual honesty, not that the four are jointly sufficient — though identifying a fifth relocation of the problem being addressed eludes me. 

That last gap deserves a precise statement rather than a shrug. The argument has three layers. **First**, intellectual dishonesty is closedness to the possibility that one is wrong, secured negatively: anyone asserting otherwise either holds that assertion open to correction, exhibiting the thing they deny is required, or does not, in which case the assertion is uninformative by §3.2. **Second**, closedness manifests in three ways, and only three so far as I can determine: avoiding the test, ignoring its verdict, and rigging the standard. **Third**, the four conditions detect all three, because each forecloses one of the four ways the defect can reappear one level up. So: **I have not shown these four are jointly sufficient. I have shown that closedness manifests in three ways, that these four conditions detect all three, and that I have been unable to identify a fourth manifestation or a fifth relocation. The enumerations are stated so that a reader can supply one.** 

Two qualifications narrow the foregoing. **Failure to practice intellectual honesty is categorical; success is evidential.** A single sustained and undefended instance of any epistemic conduct failure mode settles the matter of closedness (§3.5), but a long record showing none of the three under sustained adversarial attempts to detect otherwise does not *entail* openness; **it accumulates as evidence for it**. A conforming system therefore reports either a failure to practice intellectual honesty or a cumulative, growing confidence of successful practice — never the reverse. This is a further reason the substrate should represent degrees of confidence as opposed to binary states of assertion. **And what such a record shows is narrower than it appears.** Any actual record covers the claims that were challenged, not every claim a person holds, while §6 says there is no domain-limited intellectual honesty. There is no conflict, because the record is not a survey. What unbounded scope secures is that any claim *may* be challenged, so what the record shows is that **no contradictions have been assuredly protected**, which is more modest than that everything was examined. 

## 7.2 An existence proof, and why it is not offered as evidence

Something has been built with the intention of satisfying §5's conditions. It is described elsewhere, it is at an early stage, and **it is not offered here as evidence for anything**. Advancing a developing artifact as proof that a specification is satisfiable would be an instance of the failure this paper describes. 

What I will say is what would count as an existence proof. **The pilot's task is to determine whether the conditions can be jointly satisfied in practice at all**, which the paper cannot answer from the armchair. The results that would matter most are the ones that would embarrass the design: whether participation is cross-tribal or remains confined to one ideological slice of the population; whether verdicts **settle** at defensible values (credences) under sustained attack, or tend to settle confidently on claims later shown to be false; whether refusal-to-engage becomes the default for whichever party is losing the argument (as opposed to incentivizing a paradigm shift from caring about conclusions to caring about methods); and whether the structure accumulates mutually inconsistent settled regions of human knowledge. If the answers go the wrong way, the specification in §5 may still be correct but simply unsatisfiable, which would itself be worth knowing. 

## 7.3 Two problems I have not solved

**Validity and power come apart; only the first is addressed here.** The conditions specify what makes a check *capable* of warranting an attribution of intellectual honesty. They say nothing about how well-supported any particular reading is, which depends on how thoroughly the claims in question are being scrutinized. **A conforming system with thin participation is a valid instrument with low power**. I do not think depth and width of participation should be a fifth condition: it is not derivable from the concept, it is empirical, and no system could satisfy it at inception. It belongs as a stated limit on what the conditions buy, and it means any epistemic status such a system reports about claims (credibility) and persons (conduct) should convey its evidential base, as a statistical estimate carries its interval. 

**Some disagreements survive a fully conforming high-powered check — perhaps indefinitely.** The clearest case I know is between someone who treats individual autonomy as constitutive of what makes a life go well and someone who treats it as instrumental to welfare and therefore tradeable. Every empirical test either party proposes measures preference satisfaction, and so presupposes the welfarist metric; there is no neutral test, even in principle, because the disagreement is about which measurement counts as evidence (Chang 1997). These are Fogelin's (1985) **deep disagreements**, which Ranalli (2022) distinguishes from disagreements that merely persist. This is not a defect in the conditions: no procedure that adjudicates by evidence can settle a dispute about what evidence would bear. What a conforming system supplies here is not agreement but **legibility**: the point at which the parties part company is located exactly, and it becomes visible that they part over a standard rather than a fact. I do not know how large this class is. I have one worked example and an argument that such cases must exist, and no estimate of their proportion. The argument depends only on there being a great many disputes that are *not* of this kind. And the number of disputes not of this kind are indeed great — greater than many would assume. For example, value differences are not automatically all part of this class. Moral honesty is largely dependent upon — though not contained within — intellectual honesty. Subsidiary values can either conflict or not with professed foundational values. Foundational values that lead to self-limiting cooperation at best (or to no cooperation, at worst) are performatively not coherently defensible over and against ones that lead to non-self-limiting cooperation compatible with intellectual honesty.

## 7.5 What the argument amounts to

The claim to intellectual honesty is among the most common a person can make, and it has never carried information; made on one's own authority, it undoes itself. That is not a moral failing of the people making it. It is a fact about the instruments available, and the instruments have been missing for as long as there have been claims. 

What I have tried to give is a specification of what would be missing no longer: four conditions, each derived from the diagnosis rather than from any institution, and each answering a way the original problems in epistemic conduct can reappear one level up. 

If the specification is right, it has a consequence — that a person cannot both claim the trait of intellectual honesty and defensibly refuse an available check on it once such a check exists. 

If it is wrong, a correction would be of tremendous value. To understand why, one need only consider the following. Intellectually dishonest and cooperation-limiting strategies have no meaningful constituency in the world we live in under their own name. No one defends "dishonesty" or "anti-cooperation" in public. And yet these strategies are everywhere. **Their entire operation clearly consists of being mistaken for their opposite. The *disguise* is what is load-bearing**. A mechanism, then, that makes clear the difference between strategies, ideologies and values that are intellectually dishonest or cooperation-limiting and those that are not could increasingly weaken the disguise as it scales — with increasingly greater power to do so accurately (§7.3, ¶1). The quest to identify and build an adequate system for checking intellectual honesty could be synonymous with the quest to minimize what is perhaps the largest preventable man-made source of suffering, corruption, injustice, divisiveness and unfreedom the world knows. The first type of unfreedom it would structurally chip away at is arguably the most basic one — epistemic unfreedom.
