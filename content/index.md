Title: The Self-Certification Problem: Intellectual Honesty and the Conditions for a Warranted Attribution

Author: James Cutler

# Abstract 

Virtue epistemology analyzes intellectual virtues, honesty among them, as traits of character; the epistemology of testimony says when to accept what others tell us about the world. Neither tells us what could warrant accepting a person's testimony about her own or another's epistemic conduct. This paper takes up that case. I argue that self-certification of intellectual honesty is non-verifying, since the claim is equally available whether or not the conduct occurred, and self-defeating when offered as warranted, since it reserves exactly the adjudicative standing the practice forswears. The principle that no one may judge their own cause, settled law for tribunals and licensure, has one unexamined exception, and it is the most common implicit epistemic claim there is, inevitably infecting the structuring of power on every scale, politically and otherwise. After showing that no existing institution supplies the missing check, I derive from the diagnosis four conditions an adequate check of intellectual honesty would have to satisfy: it would have to be external to the claimant, failure-sensitive with tests the claimant does not select, objectively readable about the claimant's own conduct, and free of any party exercising discretion over identified claims. Three further properties follow from the fourth condition, and a uniqueness result: two such systems for supplying the check must share one procedure and one record, or whichever refuses to fails the condition. Once such a check is available, claiming to practice intellectual honesty while refusing it cannot be coherently defended, whatever else is offered as warrant. There are limits to such a check, including deep disagreements. The author's own attempt to build a system for such a check is disclosed and not offered as evidence for the paper’s thesis.

**Keywords:** intellectual honesty; self-certification; testimony; open-mindedness; deep disagreement; discourse ethics; social epistemology

# 1. Introduction

Almost everyone thinks it about themselves. Ask a scientist, a journalist, a partisan or even a dogmatist of any description whether they follow the evidence where it leads, and the answer is almost always yes — sincerely and often without hesitation. Indeed, how many of us can say we've ever heard an admission to the contrary — an admission of intellectual dishonesty? This assumption about oneself is so widespread that its negation is a remarkable thing to hear. 

And yet, no one has ever been able to check it. What's more, asserting it about oneself is ironically contrary to the practice of intellectual honesty. 

That contradiction and what would be required to resolve the problem are the subject of this paper. It is not a complaint about hypocrisy. My argument does not depend on anyone lying. It is not weakened by supposing that every person who has ever claimed to follow the evidence was reporting the truth as they understood it. What this paper attempts to show is that the self-reported claim carries no information of its own and is self-defeating regardless — that it would be uninformative and indefensible even in a population of scrupulous, sincere reporters — and that this is a fact about the structure of the claim rather than about the character of the people making it. 

Often the diagnosis runs the other way. The problem is that people are tribalistic, motivated reasoners, or poorly educated, and the remedy is *exhortation and instruction*: teach critical thinking, model intellectual humility, give people tools. We have pursued that remedy with considerable resources for over a century, and the results are instructive in a way the standard account cannot explain. Instruction produces real, measurable gains on tests of critical thinking,[^ct] yet the gains transfer poorly out of the classroom, and they are deployed least of all where they matter most — on the reasoner's own identity-laden beliefs. 

The capacity is teachable. What has never been *incentivized* is its application to one's core beliefs, and there is a better explanation for that than lazy students or stubborn adults: the remedy has been aimed at the wrong thing. The binding constraint was never that people struggle to reason well. Most can identify a fallacy in an opponent's argument easily enough. It is that deploying that capacity against one’s own beliefs has usually carried a social penalty, and whether it does or doesn't, there is no objective way for others to tell whether that is what you are successfully doing. Whether you end up agreeing with them is far more visible, and far too distracting. 

That absence, and what would fill it, is what this paper attempts to characterize precisely. §2 sets out the phenomenon: a claim to intellectual honesty that is made almost everywhere, at least implicitly, that carries real consequences for how credibility is allocated and thus how power is structured, and that no one has ever been in a position to verify. §3 argues that the claim is not merely non-verifying but also structurally self-defeating — that self-reported intellectual honesty cannot in principle supply evidence of the practice, and that the assertion, made on one's own authority, instantiates the very failure it denies. A record of one's own corrections does not repair this. §4 takes up the obvious reply, that we already have institutions for this. §5 asks what a valid check would have to be, and derives the requirements from the diagnosis rather than from any existing institution. §6 draws a corollary that I take to be a direct consequence of the paper. §7 separates what has been established from what has not, and sets out three problems I have not solved: that a check's validity is distinct from its power; that some disagreements are deep, though only between a restricted class of foundational commitments; and that error shared by both parties is invisible to the check. It closes with what the argument amounts to.

So far as I have been able to determine, the question — what warrants an attribution of intellectual honesty? — has been asked but not answered. Virtue epistemology has produced careful accounts of what the intellectual virtues *are* — honesty and humility as states of character among them. The epistemology of testimony has produced careful accounts of when to accept what others tell us about the world.[^gap] Neither literature says what could warrant accepting a person's claim to intellectual honesty, or what an attribution of it would have to rest on to be valid. The nearest approach is Code's. She asks how a person's epistemic responsibility could be assessed, by others or by the person herself, and answers that it cannot be codified and must be left to communal judgment, in which experts serve as arbiters (Code \[1987\] 2020, 175, 232). What no one has asked, so far as I can find, is what conditions a check on a person's own epistemic conduct would have to meet. That gap is this paper's subject. Miller's treatment of honesty comes to its edge and stops there: he names the virtue neighboring the focus of his work "*epistemic* honesty," as "primarily concerned with how someone comes to form beliefs," and then sets it aside — "there would be a lot of details that need to be sorted out in trying to make these speculations about epistemic honesty more precise. They will have to wait for another time" (Miller 2021, 111–112). What Miller calls epistemic honesty is what this paper calls intellectual honesty, and the question taken up here is one of those details: not simply what the virtue is, but what could warrant attributing it.

The nearest existing treatment is the literature on the apparent paradoxes of humility, where saying "I am humble" is thought to undercut itself, since the speaker implies knowing that she is virtuous or reveals an aim to impress (Hughes 2024, 425). Hughes (2024, 431) argues that this is no genuine paradox at all, since "pragmatic paradoxes involve contradictions, not exceptions," and a humble person can sometimes truthfully say so. The argument below is that paradox's harder relative: what defeats a claim to intellectual honesty is not what the assertion *reveals about the speaker's attitudes* but what it *reserves* — the authority to decide when an objection to one's own epistemic conduct or an argument against any truth proposition has landed. By Hughes's own criterion, this one is genuine: absent an external check, the reservation of authority is made by the claim to intellectual honesty itself, *whatever the speaker's attitudes*, and so admits *no* exceptions. 

The closest anticipation of the question itself is Bartley's. He asks, "is it possible, with intellectual integrity, to claim to be a rationalist?", and makes "the problem of how to tell a genuine from a nongenuine rationalist" central (Bartley 1984, 86). His answer is that the claim is logically coherent once criticism is separated from justification. How such a claim could be checked he names and sets aside, among "the technological problems of what means of criticism to adopt and how to organize these means, our critical intellectual institutions, so as to achieve maximum criticism" (123). This paper takes up the problem he set aside. 

The principle that no one may hold the authority to judge one’s own case is not new. *Nemo iudex in causa sua* — no one may be judge in their own cause — has been a fixture of natural justice since Roman law and *Bonham's Case* (1610), applied without controversy to tribunals, audits, and licensure. Its companion maxim, *audi alteram partem* — hear the other side — completes the pair, and a self-certification breaches both: the claimant judges her own case, and the only side others hear directly from her is her own. **Both maxims concern decisions that bind, influence or otherwise affect others,** and that is exactly the standing a self-certification — even if implicit — acquires **once credibility is allocated on it** (§2). 

Consider, as an example, a free and fair election, and the credibility its outcome confers on the epistemic conduct of the electorate responsible for it. The outcome is commonly viewed as in keeping with the responsible structuring of power by virtue of its democratic nature, regardless of how unjustified and disastrous some of its downstream effects seem to many of the people affected. This holds on every side of every election in polarized countries. Each voter who contributed to the outcome would nonetheless likely self-certify their epistemic conduct as virtuous, and the outcome's downstream effects as justified relative to the counterfactual alternative. But an election settles who governs, not whether what the majority chose can be justified. That gap is related to the familiar concept of the tyranny of the majority, but it is not quite the same. That worry concerns what a majority may do to a minority, and its remedies — enumerated rights, separation of powers, judicial review — constrain what the majority may do (Madison 1787; Tocqueville 1835; Mill 1859). None of those remedies reaches how the majority's will was formed, and they are staffed through that will — e.g., the president nominates judges, the Senate confirms them; crucially, elections fill *both* (see Levinson and Pildes 2006). Nor do they reach those outside the domestic population altogether. Where one nation's foreign policy is consequential enough, the people who bear its costs have no vote, and there is usually no forum in which the decision owes them an answer.[^iraq] What might seem a harmless self-certification reaches the lives of a great many people, often dramatically, even through something as virtuous as democracy, given how directly epistemic conduct is linked to voting behavior.[^vote] Undemocratic power is a starker case still: where power is concentrated rather than distributed, a few self-certifications reach everyone. None of this means the right to vote should become an earned credential, and nothing here proposes making it one. It is in light of cases such as these, however, that this paper examines the one place the two Latin maxims have always been needed yet never applied: a nearly universal claim, implicit or explicit, of virtuous epistemic conduct.

It may seem odd to devote a paper to the point that self-certification is worthless, since everyone already knows it. No one self-certifies as a surgeon; a licensing board does it. No one self-certifies as a pilot, an accountant, or a point guard; there are examinations, audits, and tryouts, each built precisely so that the person's own say-so would not have to be taken. **Wherever a claim about competence or conduct matters enough for others to allocate trust on it**, we have built an instrument that removes the claimant from the position of sole witness. The observation of this paper is that intellectual honesty is the exception: a claim (often implicit) on which credibility is allocated constantly, at every scale in society, and for which no such instrument has ever existed. Self-certification is not a novel problem. It is a solved problem virtually everywhere except here. 

Three clarifications about scope: 

First, I am concerned throughout with *attributions and practice*, not with dispositions. The question is not whether anyone possesses the inner virtue of intellectual honesty, but whether the attribution of the *practice* to a person can be warranted, and by what. I take intellectual honesty to be something one does rather than something one has, and something done with others rather than alone, in the way that one plays a match, distinct from shadow-boxing; §3.6 says why this matters. The reader who prefers a dispositional account (Roberts and Wood 2007; Whitcomb et al. 2017) will find that the argument survives, since it concerns what would warrant *attributing* the trait, whichever analysis of the trait is correct. §5.2 makes this explicit.

I do commit to one constraint on the analyses available, and I state it here rather than let it emerge unannounced. Whatever else intellectual honesty consists in, it cannot be compatible with closedness to the possibility that one is wrong. This is a constraint on the space of admissible accounts rather than a complete account of my own; it rules out a class without selecting a member, and I take it to be one that a virtue epistemologist and a practice theorist can accept on the same terms. §3.6 secures it. 

Second, I claim nothing about anyone's blameworthiness, and the argument in fact runs the other way. If mere self-certified or self-monitored intellectual honesty can only be self-defeating (as I will attempt to show), and no institution has ever happened to supply the conditions to make the attribution coherently defensible either, then everyone who has ever asserted their own (aspiration to) intellectual honesty has been doing the only thing available, at no fault of their own. The universality of the self-reported claim and the universality of its unverifiability are the same fact, from two different angles. If a claim can't be checked, nothing stops anyone from making it. So virtually everyone does. 

Third, three questions travel together here and should be kept apart: whether a person *is* practicing intellectual honesty; whether she is *warranted in believing* that she is; and whether *others* are warranted in attributing it to her. This paper's focus is the third. §3.3's argument bears on the second as well, since a person keeping her own record faces the same denominator problem as anyone reading it. The first is touched only where I specify, in §3.6 and §3.7, and what I say there naturally depends on the practice account I adopt. The argument about warrant does not depend on that account. 

# 2. The self-certification problem

Consider what happens when someone's intellectual honesty is at issue. 

It is at issue constantly in public discourse, even if at times only below the surface of disputes. Public disagreement is not, for the most part, a dispute about the same set of first-order facts that the participants have carefully weighed together and yet somehow read differently. It is a dispute about who is reasoning honestly and who is not. That is why the accusation is so common (even if only implied), in both directions, and why it is so rarely — if ever — resolved. Each party takes the other to be motivated in their reasoning; each takes themselves to be following the evidence; **and there is no procedure whatever in public discourse for settling the matter.** 

What is sincerely offered in place of a procedure is testimony, from the person whose conduct is in question. 

I will call this **self-certification**: the practice, quite common because nothing else is available, of an agent supplying the evidence for their own virtuous epistemic conduct. It takes three forms. Most commonly, bare assertion — *I follow the evidence; I have no agenda (or if I have one, I won't let it get in the way); I would change my mind if the evidence demanded it*. Second, a record of past corrections, offered as demonstration. Third, an appeal to method: *I am a scientist, I hold myself to the standards of my field* — which is a claim about one's own conformity to a standard, and is therefore similarly self-supplied, in addition to being domain-limited. 

The consequences are not academic. Credibility is allocated on the persuasiveness of these self-certifications — even if subconscious — in the minds of different audiences. Credibility then shapes collective behavior and the structuring of power from the smallest to the largest scales in society. Where the certifications are unverifiable and mutually contradictory — as they always are in disagreements, since both parties issue them and only issue them when there is a disagreement — the pattern of allocation falls back on the only remaining signals available: prior agreement, group membership, institutional affiliation, or confidence of delivery.[^fricker] Whatever else those are, they are not measures of epistemic conduct. 

Durable power rests on opinion. As Hume put it, "It is therefore, on opinion only that government is founded; and this maxim extends to the most despotic and most military governments, as well as to the most free and most popular" (Hume 1741). Opinion, in turn, rests largely on credibility: most of what people believe about government concerns things they never witness themselves; they believe it on others' say-so.[^hume] Allocating credibility is itself epistemic conduct: deciding which claims to put stock in, and why. So the problem is not confined to those who claim honesty. It reaches everyone who forms beliefs on others' say-so, which is nearly everyone. Honest or virtuous epistemic conduct cannot currently be rewarded, because there is no objectively readable way to tell whether it is there (§5.3, condition (c)). That would be so even if nearly everyone were inclined to intellectual honesty, since good will cannot check itself (§3.1). The stakes, though, are highest where that inclination is hardest to assume: amid polarization and conflict, within nations and between them, and under authoritarianism, which closes disputes by fiat rather than by reason (§5.3).

That self-certification of intellectual honesty is unreliable is a familiar complaint and not mine. People are poor judges of their own susceptibility to motivated reasoning; the literature on that is large and I take it as read.[^judges] My claim is different in kind: self-certification of intellectual honesty is structurally non-verifying and self-defeating. It would remain self-defeating even if everyone issuing it were both sincere and careful. Reporter quality is not the variable that has failed. 

That is the argument of the next section. 

# 3. Why self-certification is structurally non-verifying and self-defeating 

## 3.1 Two claims, distinguished

§2 separated the familiar complaint from mine. The familiar complaint is that self-reported intellectual honesty is **unreliable**: the signal is often wrong. If that were the whole problem, the remedy would be more rigorous introspection or a stronger norm of self-awareness, and the problem would be difficult but not structural. 

Mine is that self-reported intellectual honesty is **structurally defective**, and in two ways. It is **non-verifying**: the utterance itself carries no information about the underlying state, and would carry none even if every reporter were perfectly sincere and striving for greater rigor. And asserting it as warranted is **self-defeating**: made on the claimant's own authority, the assertion reserves exactly the adjudicative standing that the practice it asserts forswears, so that issuing it instantiates the failure it denies. 

That the argument does not turn on anyone's insincerity matters, because the natural response to the first charge — exhort people to be more honest about their intellectual dishonesty — has been tried continuously for a century, and the second charge explains why it could not have worked.

Nor would better motivation repair it. Suppose everyone wanted, for its own sake, to hold their claims answerable to reason. Even fully motivated people would still need a way to know they are succeeding. **A sincere person cannot tell from the inside whether they have heard the other side or only think they have**, because the step most exposed to motivated reasoning is judging whether an objection has landed (§5.3). Motivation without a check is still self-certification. The problem is not a shortage of good will, and good will cannot solve it.

§§3.2–3.3 establish the first half, non-verifiability. The second half, self-defeat, waits until §3.7, since stating it precisely requires machinery this section has yet to build. 

## 3.2 Self-attribution as cheap talk 

A signal carries information about a state of the world only if its probability differs across the states it is supposed to discriminate. If a signal is equally likely whether or not the state obtains, observing it tells you nothing you did not already know. 

Consider what a claim to intellectual honesty costs. It is available to anyone, at any time, for nothing. The scrupulously honest inquirer can say *I follow the evidence where it leads*; so can the inquirer whose every conclusion is fixed in advance. Nothing about producing the utterance requires having done what it reports. Contrast this with other types of assertions: “I can fly an airplane,” “I can slam dunk a basketball,” “I have a PhD,” “I have a black belt in jiu jitsu,” “I can bench 300 lbs,” “I’m proficient in calculus.” Each of these claims requires being able to do or having done what the claims report, or to seriously and needlessly risk being exposed, at least eventually. And the same could be said about a tremendous number of other claims people are only *likely* to make or assume about themselves if they’re true. Not so for the claim to intellectual honesty. How would one verify such a thing, after all? This difference relies on a structural fact, and it should be stated carefully, because it is easy to overstate. I am not asserting, as an empirical matter, that the two kinds of inquirer — honest and dishonest — utter the sentence at exactly equal rates; that would require measuring both, and I have measured neither. The claim is that the utterance imposes no *state-dependent constraint*: it is equally available to both types, at the same cost, with the same apparent sincerity. Any correlation between saying it and doing it would therefore have to arise from contingent features of a population — who happens to be modest, who happens to be self-aware — and not from anything the claim itself does. In the idealized case where such contingencies wash out, the likelihood ratio is one: 

$$
P(\text{claim} \, \mid \, \text{intellectually honest}) = P(\text{claim} \, \mid \, \text{not intellectually honest}) \approx 1 \, \Rightarrow \, LR = 1
$$

In every actual case, whatever the population is like, the utterance itself contributes nothing to whatever ratio obtains. The signal is what economists call cheap talk: an utterance with no cost differential across the states it purports to distinguish, and therefore with no discriminating power of its own. The formal result is Crawford and Sobel's: where the interests of sender and receiver diverge, costless messages transmit only coarse information, and past a threshold of divergence they transmit none at all.[^cs] 

Again, this is not a defect of any particular claimant. It is a property of the utterance type, and it is why, applied to intellectual honesty, I call self-certification *non-verifying*: it carries no information about the state *through the claim itself*. The empirical literature on adjacent virtues supplies the expected complement rather than a counterexample: self-ratings of intellectual humility agree only weakly with "informant" ratings (ratings from people chosen by the subjects as people who know the subject well), which is what one would predict of a signal available to everyone.[^ihm] 

A clarification is needed here, since the natural inference from what I've said is that a credible signal must be an expensive one, and that is not borne out by the literature. What sustains informative signaling is not that the honest signaler pays a high price, but that *deviation would be costly*. Consider a company audit. A business with accurate books hands over records it was keeping anyway — near zero cost. A business cooking its books is the one that must pay the cost of fabricating a parallel set of internally consistent records or get caught. In this context, realized cost at an equilibrium of intellectual honesty may be near zero: the person who would have revised their view anyway pays nothing to be seen revising it. The cost falls on whoever would hold the signal without doing the thing it reports, and it falls on them at the point of deviation. I return to this in §5, where I constrain what an adequate check would have to look like; here it matters only as a caution against reading the diagnosis as a demand for costly display. It is not. 

## 3.3 Why a record of corrections does not repair it

The obvious reply is that some people do more than assert intellectual honesty. They keep a record. They publicly acknowledge their errors, and the acknowledgments accumulate into evidence of a disposition. Surely a demonstrated history of correction is not cheap talk. 

Let $n$ be the number of errors in a person's public discourse **that others have identified**, and $k$ the number that the claimant has publicly acknowledged. A corrections record is an implicit claim that $k/n$ is high — that when shown to be wrong, this person acknowledges as much. 

The difficulty is that **the claimant actually supplies *both* terms**. They decide which criticisms rose to the level of meriting a response, which is prior to $n$ as the claimant reports it. They then decide which of those objections land, which *is* the only $n$ the claimant accepts, by definition. Then they decide to acknowledge the error, $k$. This makes it easy to keep $k/n$ equal to $1$ — a 100% track record of updating in response to evidence — if they want, which is the whole idea. Who would report an error they’ve elected not to correct? 

Importantly, and structurally, no one else is deciding what $n$ is *for the claimant’s own record*, nor can they while the record remains the claimant's. That's the whole disagreement that makes this ratio meaningless. The person certifying their own intellectual honesty has no choice but to adjudicate whether an objection to something they've claimed has landed. If an observer disagrees and has a larger $n$ for the claimant, the additional errors structurally cannot enter into the claimant's own $n$ register without the claimant agreeing that they are in fact errors. No one can force the claimant to agree, and doing so would be self-defeating anyway. Any criticism the claimant ignores or incorrectly dismisses simply cannot appear in their denominator. Self-monitoring is unavoidable.[^korn] And crucially, it does not matter how sincere the claimant's belief is that a given criticism fails. 

This holds with full force even when every acknowledged correction is genuine. Suppose a person's corrections are scrupulously accurate, generously worded, and promptly issued. The record is still uninformative about the ratio it is offered as evidence for, because the denominator was never determined by anyone but its author. Just such an author could in fact nonetheless be grossly disconnected in at least one domain of truth claims from a whole body of evidence they have mistakenly determined not to examine, or which they have utterly failed to examine carefully and fairly enough (a validity issue, as explained in §7.2). Or the author could be woefully unaware of such a body of evidence, simply because their epistemic bubble makes such evidence unlikely to reach them (a power issue, also raised in §7.2). Either way, the point is that a perfectly truthful numerator over a *structurally* unverifiable denominator yields no meaningful fraction at all. Three claims should be kept apart here, and only the third is mine: that no denominator free of the claimant's own discretion exists in principle; that no denominator can be constructed from outside; and that a self-curated denominator cannot establish what it is offered to establish. The first two are false, and §5 depends on their being false. Only the third claim is true.

The point generalizes past any individual failing. The reason no one has ever produced a credible record of their own responsiveness is not that people are unwilling. It is that no one has ever been in a position to do otherwise. There has never existed a mechanism by which one person could check another's denominator across the whole of that person's claims, unmediated by adjudicative authority. Domain-local externally imposed denominators exist — retractions, failed forecasts — and §4 examines them; but a record kept under any of them is no longer self-certification, and each stops at its domain's edge. The absence is not a shortfall of virtue; it is a gap in the available instruments.

Nor can the defect be engineered away from inside. Suppose the claimant, anticipating all of this, adopts criteria for what counts as a landed objection generally or domain-specifically for all the domains of claims made — drafted carefully, in collaboration with a panel of experts, and however valid in principle. The criteria still have to be applied to particular cases, and the applier is still the claimant. Motivated interpretation operates on the application of criteria exactly as it operated on the raw judgments the criteria were meant to discipline. This merely provides personal discretion with new targets; it does not remove it. 

## 3.4 What an external check would have to detect

If self-report structurally cannot provide what it claims to provide, the question becomes what an *external* check of intellectual honesty would have to look for. I suggest three failure modes, each of which a competent check must be able to detect, either independently of the others or as a derivation from them. 

All three of them are different manifestations of one underlying principle, however: **exemption**. Exemption is refusing to be open to the possibility that a claim might be wrong. The claim is exempted from that possibility. 

1. The first is **evasion.** When exemption manifests as evasion, a claim about objective reality is shielded from scrutiny. Behaviorally this takes the form of declining scrutiny or avoiding scrutiny of one’s claim. Exposure to scrutiny or to a conflicting perspective and its rationale is what is avoided here. We could call this 'avoiding the test.' 

2. The second is **unresponsiveness.** When exemption manifests as unresponsiveness, one leaves one's confidence where it was when evidence one has accepted as bearing on the position implies rather that it should move, while offering no defeater that survives. Evidence that is redundant with what one already held, evidence whose source one has shown unreliable, and evidence already priced in are defeaters, and a person who states one has not been unresponsive. A person who states nothing has.[^hoe] With unresponsiveness, a person is not evading or shielding their claim from scrutiny. They're subjecting the claim to the test but ignoring its verdict.

3. The third is **inconsistency.** When exemption manifests as an inconsistent evidence standard, one demands of others' claims a standard of rigor that one does not apply to structurally matched claims of one's own. In other words, what is exempted is not one's own claim outright, but exemption of it from a fair degree of rigor or justification. This can occur in a latent, unconscious, inadvertent way, such that the person is not genuinely aware of their own inconsistency, requiring implications about inconsistency that differ from the previous two failure modes. We could call this failure mode 'rigging the standard,' in contrast with avoiding the test or ignoring its verdict. 

The fact that all three are different manifestations of one thing — exemption — can potentially lead to some loss of nuance. The first two are functionally independent. Consider an inquirer who appears at every challenge, engages at length, argues with energy and good humor, accepts along the way that at least some of what is put to her bears on her position, and never once revises a position or states a surviving defeater. She avoids the first failure mode (evasion) and commits the second. I suspect this is the commonest sophisticated form. 

Consider next an inquirer who revises readily whenever evidence does happen to reach him, but who never voluntarily submits a claim for examination and/or never answers an invitation to open discussion on a topic he doesn't deep down feel epistemically secure about. He avoids the second failure mode (unresponsiveness) but commits the first. 

Consider finally an inquirer who never evades, revises whenever the evidence obliges, but seeks to reserve her demands for rigor exclusively for positions she opposes. Her standard of what counts as evidence is inconsistent. She avoids the first two failure modes, in other words, but has a tendency to commit the third (unwittingly, perhaps). Can she do so under sustained adversarial enforcement of the first two norms? Not if the first two checks are in fact satisfied. If she makes demands of others' claims that she does not meet herself, they are naturally motivated to press the matched demand back on her and to name and challenge the standard as a claim in its own right. Since a standard of evidence *is* itself a claim, it is subject to the first two checks like any other. 

Thus, as long as a system applies the first two conditions recursively — by applying them to demands and standards as well as to first-order claims — it will detect the third failure mode without needing to enforce it separately. (The recursion is not an independent condition. §5 derives it from other conditions an adequate check must meet.) So the three are not three independent vices. They are the three ways the same thing (intellectual dishonesty) shows itself, and the third is the one a check gets for free once the first two are enforced at every level. 

## 3.5 Why each failure is categorical rather than a matter of degree

At this point a natural retreat presents itself. Everyone, it will be said, falls short on all three counts to some extent. Intellectual honesty is an ideal that no one fully attains, and what we are describing is a spectrum along which people are distributed. If so, the analysis yields a ranking rather than a verdict, and the interesting question becomes who ranks where. 

I will resist this, firstly, because each of the three failures has a structure that a spectrum interpretation obfuscates. In each case the behavior presupposes a rule, and in each case the rule cannot be defended. Secondly, it is not true that everyone has to fall short, because it is always within one's power to *never* commit the first two failure modes — to never decline scrutiny and to never refuse to update once evidence with a discriminatory likelihood ratio is defensibly introduced to them. And it is always within one's power to never persist in holding an inconsistent evidence standard once the contradiction has been defensibly identified and brought to their attention. In other words, it is always within one's power to practice intellectual honesty with zero identified and persisting failures, including none of the first two failure modes *at all*, even if it is beyond human achievement to entirely remove *psychological* bias. Practicing intellectual honesty and removing psychological bias are two entirely separate things. This includes the fact that persistently holding to an inconsistent evidence standard once it is brought to one’s attention and psychological or emotional bias — epistemic preferences — are entirely separable. One can have a strong desire for their sports team to win tonight. That by no means obligates them to reject the final score if it says their team lost. And the score can be *methodologically* *un*biased. 

For the first reason to resist a spectrum interpretation of intellectual honesty, my argument has a common form: 

>A given behavior presupposes a rule $R$. $R$ is not coherently defensible. Acting on a presupposition whose defense is self-defeating is incompatible with practicing intellectual honesty. Therefore the behavior is incompatible with the practice. 

For example: to persistently decline scrutiny of a claim without offering a defeasible reason presupposes that the claim is exempt from rational scrutiny of the kind appropriate to it. To hold a credence unmoved by evidence one has accepted presupposes that the jurisdiction of justification does not extend to oneself. To demand rigor of others that one does not apply to one's own matched claims presupposes that rigor is owed by others and not by oneself. None of these rules survives being stated, let alone universalized.

A second retreat is more instructive. Grant that failure to practice intellectual honesty is strictly categorical. An inquirer may still say: I hold nothing exempt — except one thing. On every other commitment I revise when the evidence demands it, readily and often. Surely a practice that reaches all but one of a person's claims still doesn’t belong in the category of failed epistemic conduct. 

The reply is perhaps the most crucial part of this paper: **an exemption is not a subtraction but a complete re-description**, and treating it as a subtraction mistakes what an exemption is. 

To hold a claim exempt from scrutiny is to hold that it need not answer to evidence, and something must have the standing to decide that. There are two possibilities. The standing may not be the inquirer's. She may hold that reasons of a general kind — about the sort of claim this is, or the sort of assessment it admits of — establish that it is not answerable in the way others are. But then those reasons are open to challenge, and whatever established the exemption can withdraw it. Thus, she has not exempted anything; she has produced an answerable conclusion about how a claim should be assessed. Or the standing may be her own. If the exemption rests on nothing beyond her decision to exempt, that standing is not confined to the claim it is exercised on. It is available for all the other claims she holds to, now. And what is to stop anyone else from making the same decision, likewise about whichever claim(s) they choose? 

Two things follow, and it is important to keep them apart. The first is about the present: a person maintaining such an exemption is currently conducting the whole of her practice under a rule that licenses exempting anything, whether or not she has exercised it elsewhere, in the way that an official who holds one irrevocable immunity holds office on different terms from her colleagues even if she never invokes it. The second is about the past, and it is evidential rather than metaphysical: her many revisions do not show that she was bound by evidence, because a person operating under the optional policy produces exactly the same revisions wherever the evidence points where she was willing to go anyway. The record of revisions is not falsified; it is rendered uninformative about the thing it was offered to show. The exemption does not remove one item from the scope of the practice. It discloses the terms on which the whole of it is being conducted. Only the second possibility — exempting a claim on the standing of one’s own authority or self-granted privilege — is an exemption in the sense at issue, and one question distinguishes them wherever they are met: what could withdraw it, and by what procedure?[^exempt]

The structure is the one familiar from the rule of law, which admits both kinds of exemption — the superficial and the real: 

1. **Diplomatic immunity.** This exempts a class of persons from ordinary process without the rules-based order ceasing to be one: the exemption is granted by law, its extent is defined in law, and it is revocable by the same process that could repeal any other law. The locus of sovereignty has not moved. It is still located in rationally answerable rules rather than rulers. 
2. **Dictatorship.** What destroys a rules-based order is an exemption no law granted and no law can revoke, whose extent is set by the person holding it — by the dictator. Every judgment the order issues is then issued under an authority that could have suspended it, and everyone else's compliance stands revealed as subject to the whim of the one person exempt from it. Such an exemption does not subtract a millionth from the rule of law; it completely relocates sovereignty. Everyone else's law-abiding conduct may be unchanged, but it has stopped being the relevant fact about the system. 

In epistemology: 

**(1) A class of truth claims is “exempted” for coherently supplied reasons.** 

Offering a reason for exempting a claim logically does two things. 

First, it applies by virtue of some feature of the claim — e.g., that it is sacred, or foundational, or not amenable to ordinary methods — a feature that defines a class, because a reason that holds for this claim holds for every claim with that feature. “It is sacred” exempts every claim held sacred, in every tradition; “it was revealed” exempts every claim anyone says was revealed to them; “it is foundational” exempts whatever anyone treats as foundational. Nothing in the reason can keep contradictory claims out of the class, and any further reason offered for keeping them out would itself be answerable, as the second consequence shows. One of the rules of rationality is non-contradiction. When we make exemptions to holding claims rationally answerable, we remove the only means of enforcing that rule among them, since no reason could ever require an exempted claim to give way, allowing for contradiction within the class of claims exempted that no rational analysis could remedy, by virtue of that very exemption — not because reason has nothing to say, but because it has been barred from saying it. 

Second, providing a reason undermines the notion that the claim is truly exempt. As long as the reason is genuinely offered, the implication is that the reason — the whole basis for the exemption — is subject to rational scrutiny, and thus in principle overturnable by it, otherwise it is no reason at all. Thus, as long as the entire basis for the exemption is answerable to reason, the exemption is only an illusion. Its entire basis is *not* exempted — it must remain answerable to reason. 

Combining the two consequences, we get a more catastrophic result than either suggests by itself. The fact that the exemption allows for contradiction shielded from rational scrutiny fatally undermines the viability of whatever reason was given. *No matter how strong one thinks that reason is*, it logically cannot overcome the fact that the reason displaced rationality itself by allowing for such contradiction among the class of claims exempted by it without remedy. Crucially, this holds whatever the reason is, because of what it exempts, so the reasons one might think of need not be examined one by one. Correctly applied, the rules of rationality cannot, by definition, warrant both a claim and its contradiction, or shield the contradiction between them from reason.  

**(2) A true exemption.** 

Offering no reason for the exemption — simply announcing it — inevitably makes the exemption rest purely on the standing of one’s own authority. This implies a generalizable rule about exemption whether one wants it to or not. To deny such a thing without reasons is just another exemption that anyone else could similarly arrogate to themselves. To object to this on the basis of reasons — to give a reason why I may exempt claims and others may not — is to give up exemption without reason and return to case 1. And that is precisely the rule: if I am entitled to exempt a claim from rational scrutiny without reason then what’s stopping anyone else from doing the same? The answer is, by definition: nothing. And what claims can be exempted? Since no reason for the exemption is required, any claim can be exempted. This unavoidably generalizable rule thus likewise allows for contradiction among the claims exempted by it shielded from reason. A true exemption therefore fails for the same reason case 1 does, without offering a reason that could be scrutinized. 

One may object that merely allowing for contradiction does not commit a contradiction. That is true, and it is not the charge. But, if contradiction is allowed and shielded from remedy, what that means is that there is nothing unacceptable about contradiction: once one arises, it can simply stand. Denying the epistemic perniciousness of contradiction — refusing to disallow it epistemically — is not coherently defensible. A rule under which p and not-p can both stand as warranted does not distinguish warranted beliefs from unwarranted ones at all. It is not a weak rule for identifying warrant but an invalid one. 

And rational agents practicing intellectual honesty together must not adopt rules that let contradictions between their conclusions stand shielded from reason, by rule rather than by chance, otherwise collaboratively practicing intellectual honesty would be *compatible* with such contradiction — arguably the most nonsensical result from collaboratively practicing intellectual honesty imaginable. Honest disagreement is not this, and neither is deep disagreement (§7.2), where nothing either party offers as favoring one view over the other could be admitted by the other. In both, every claim at issue remains answerable, and any reason that bore on it would move it. What a deep disagreement lacks is a reason, not permission to seek one. And the rules warrant neither side: the shared record stays at indifference and locates where the parties part (§5.6), rather than warranting both a claim and its contradiction. Nor can a deep disagreement persist over anything that observation available to both parties, or logic, bears on. Objective reality and logic do not contradict themselves, so rival claims about them cannot both keep surviving honest scrutiny (§7.2). In those realms, a contradiction that survives honest mutual scrutiny indefinitely is one that something is shielding from it. At the very least, whatever else it cannot be compatible with, collaboratively practicing intellectual honesty cannot be compatible with contradiction shielded from reason.

None of this is a claim that intellectual honesty requires never unwittingly lapsing in one’s commitment to self-consistent evidence standards (avoiding the third failure mode). A lapse corrected when surfaced is not an exemption; it is the practice working. An exemption is what a lapse becomes *when it is maintained after having been surfaced*, and §3.6's second clarification says exactly when that threshold is crossed. The ordinary observation that virtuous people sometimes fail to manifest their virtue is therefore not an objection here. A lie told and subsequently retracted is a past event with a current correction; a dogma held is a present rule without a coherent defense.[^closed] “Conventional” honesty (for lack of a better word) — i.e., being truthful; refraining from lying — and intellectual honesty thus play by different rules. A person who has lied once or twice and since confessed does deserve to have their conduct evaluated on a spectrum. No such grounds for excuse exist for persistently, knowingly holding dogmas in the present when given the opportunity to let go of them. 

One qualification, and it bears on what exactly is being charged. Nothing here faults anyone for *unobtrusively* holding a belief they cannot defend *for reasons of unfalsifiability*. A person who holds such a belief, acknowledges it is unfalsifiable, and refrains from asserting it as true — as distinct from **mere belief** — or binding on others has made no demand on anyone, and there is no failure of intellectual honesty in that. What produces the failure is a specific conjunction: asserting a claim as true, which is to imply that anyone practicing intellectual honesty should come to believe it, while holding the claim exempt from rational scrutiny appropriate to the claim’s kind. Truth claims can thus differ from mere beliefs even though they overlap, and the same conjunction reappears as the corollary in §6. Merely holding an apparently unfalsifiable belief does not require exempting it from scrutiny that might show it to be falsifiable and likely false. 

This is why the quantitative or spectrum reading of intellectual honesty is at once so natural and so misleading. On most of their substantive commitments most people probably do revise when the evidence requires it, and if intellectual honesty were an unweighted quantity, high scores would be the norm if not virtually universal. But it would take only a small number of exemptions, placed on the claims people care most about and with vast downstream epistemic effects, to leave two lucid, good-natured people living in divergent realities. I suspect much of what is called post-truth politics has this shape. 

What §4 has occasion to note is that the small number of consequential exemptions are hardly distributed at random; they fall on precisely those claims that no external process adjudicates — the claims most apt to become bound up with identity. These are the claims over which public disagreement is most consequential and least tractable. 

## 3.6 The terminal premise

The three-part schema in §3.4 rests on a premise that underwrites all three manifestations of intellectual dishonesty (evasion, unresponsiveness and inconsistency), and it deserves separate treatment, since a reader who rejects it rejects the whole argument. 

Here again is the central part of the argument from §3.5. Call it (P):

>**(P)** Acting on a presupposition whose defense is self-defeating is incompatible with practicing intellectual honesty. 

Two clarifications before the defense. First, *whose defense is self-defeating* is not *for which no proof exists*. Our most basic and most apparently successful inferential commitments, induction chief among them, admit of no non-circular defense. (Whether rule-circular defenses are pernicious or potentially benign remains contested; see Alston 1986.) If (P) targeted those it would follow that no one can ever be intellectually honest. But a rule-circular defense and a self-defeating one (e.g., premise-circularity) are different defects. The first fails to persuade someone who does not already accept the rule; the second destroys the conclusion in the act of offering it. (P) targets only the second.

Second, *acting on*. Merely harboring unexamined presuppositions is the human condition and no one's failing. As has been pointed out multiple times already, this applies to unexamined inconsistent evidence standards. It does not apply when one is offered to subject their standards or other claims to scrutiny and persistently refuses, or accepts but is unresponsive to the evidence introduced. Thus, (P) engages when the presupposition is operative in the formation or maintenance of a belief *and* its indefensibility has been surfaced. This keeps (P) from requiring omniscience about one's own commitments. 

(P) can be derived by two independent routes, and the independence is the point: a premise derivable more than one way is differently situated from one stipulated once. 

**By retortion.** Retortion exposes what Apel calls a *performative self-contradiction*: a conflict between what a claim says and what asserting it presupposes (Apel 1975). Suppose someone denies (P). 
- The denial of (P) says: one may practice intellectual honesty while acting on presuppositions whose defense is self-defeating. To _defend_ that denial is to offer it as coherently defensible, which is to treat coherent defensibility as something one's own presuppositions must meet. That is precisely what (P) says. So the act of defending the denial instantiates the standard the denial rejects. That makes defending the denial self-defeating. 
- If one does not defend one’s denial of (P), they have announced rather than argued, and the position becomes an exemption, thus defeating the practice (§3.5).

Either they supply the grounds and concede the standard, or they exit. 

**By the ontology of the practice.** Intellectual honesty, on the account I take to be correct, is not a property one possesses but a practice one engages in — **the practice of self-consistently holding one's objective truth claims *demonstrably* answerable to reasons**: answerable to objections that can actually reach them, with the response on record. If that is what the practice consists in, two things follow. Acting on a presupposition that is by construction unanswerable to reasons is not a forbidden instance of the practice; it is simply not the practice. And holding one's claims open in private, where no objection can reach them and no response is recorded, is not a diminished instance of it either. It is an attempt at the practice, and §3.3 showed that the person attempting it cannot tell whether the constitutive step was ever performed. On this route (P) is not a rule constraining the activity from the outside; it is a consequence of what the activity is. 

I should mark what depends on the practice account and what does not. The conditions of §5 and the corollary of §6 concern what would *warrant* an attribution, and go through on a dispositional account unchanged (§5.2). What depends on the practice account is the further gloss, in §3.7 and §6.3, that private openness is an attempt at the practice rather than an instance of it. A reader who rejects the practice account keeps every result and loses only that gloss. 

It may be objected that (P) is a definition smuggled in as a premise. The charge, properly understood, is one of arbitrariness — that the premise was selected because it yields the wanted result, and that a different selection was equally available. It was not. (P) constrains what intellectual honesty can be without offering an account of what it is, and the constraint is not one I was free to choose. It is reachable by the routes above, neither of which presupposes it, and its contrary cannot be coherently defended, since to assert it is either to submit the assertion to the standard it denies (by defending it) or to decline to argue at all and claim an exemption, with the contradictory results in §3.5. A premise one is not free to coherently defend rejecting is the opposite of a stipulation. 

And it may be objected that someone might act on an indefensible presupposition and arrive at the truth. So they might; the practice is constituted by how beliefs are held rather than by whether they happen to be correct. Arriving at truth by a route one cannot defend is not an instance of practicing intellectual honesty. It is merely happening to be right, without a reliable method or evidence to support the claim, or a coherent way for it to be epistemically binding on anyone else. 

Each of §3.4's failure modes is underwritten by (P), since §3.5 showed each to rest on a rule that cannot be defended. Unresponsiveness is an exemption in disguise: to subject a claim to scrutiny and then refuse to adjust one's credence to the outcome is not to have subjected it at all. 

## 3.7 What follows

Intellectual honesty is not merely difficult to verify. Self-report cannot in principle verify it (§3.2), and a corrections record from the claimant cannot repair this, since the claimant supplies both of its terms (§3.3). What is more, self-report presupposes a rule that cannot be defended: if I may self-certify my intellectual honesty, so may everyone, and the attribution then tracks nothing. §6 leans on this rule's failure; it is established here. 

And there is a stronger way to put the whole of it. **A self-certified claim to intellectual honesty is a counterexample to itself.** To see why, distinguish two things a person may be doing in saying *I follow the evidence where it leads*. She may be offering an opinion about herself with no claim to warrant, as one might say *I think I am a decent cook*. If we interpret that as not self-defeating then it is merely empty when applied to intellectual honesty, for §3.2's reason, and its honest form is to say less (§6.4). Or she may be offering it as *warranted*, as something others should credit and allocate credibility on, which is the form §2 showed does the work in public disagreement. It is the second form that is non-empty and therefore self-defeats. A reader may propose a third form between these two: *I have some reason to think I respond honestly to criticism of my views; my assessment is fallible, and a demonstrated pattern of evasion would count against it; my saying so settles nothing for you.* That is offered as warranted to the claimant herself, but modestly, and it claims no epistemic standing for you. 

It does not escape, however, and seeing why sharpens the argument. Ask what the "some reason" is. If it is a record the speaker has kept, it is §3.3's self-curated denominator, and the verdict-on-objections-landing problem applies to the grounds exactly as it applied to the bare claim. If it is something external, a check that reached her conduct, then it is not self-certification at all, and the paper has no quarrel with it. Indeed, an adequate external check is precisely what is proposed in §5. *Fallible* and *does not settle it* are disclaimers about the *strength* of the warrant; self-defeat is about its *source*. A modest self-certification is still a self-certification. To claim the practice is to claim that one holds one's claims demonstrably answerable *to reasons*. The claim about one's own conduct is a claim like any other, so it too must be answerable. But the only warrant available for it, absent any external check, is the claimant's own verdict on whether the objections to her conduct or claims have landed, which is the question at issue. To offer that verdict as warrant is not to report a fallible opinion; it is to act on the rule that one's own verdict on the question suffices to claim the practice. The point does not depend on the claimant *believing* her judgment final, and it does not ask anyone to surrender first-person judgment, which everyone retains and must retain.[^dissent] It depends only on this: where nothing but the claimant's own verdict determines whether an overturning has occurred, offering that verdict as warrant treats it as an adjudicative authority whether or not anyone intends to. An objection may persuade her, or persuade an audience over her resistance; but whether it has *landed* is, absent any external check, once again her call as far as her self-certification is concerned, and whether the audience was wrong to be persuaded is the allocation of credibility that §2 described. The reservation is structural, not psychological. To self-certify in this form is to hold exactly one claim — the claim about one's own conduct — ultimately answerable *to no one but oneself*. §5.3 elaborates further on the incompatibility of authority and intellectual honesty. 

Run §3.5's analysis on that reservation. The self-certified claim presupposes a rule: *my own verdict on whether an objection has landed settles (for me) the matter of my epistemic conduct.* That is an exemption, and of the second kind, because the standing behind it is nobody's but the claimant's — nothing beyond their own decision holds the exemption in place, and nothing but their own decision could withdraw it. The rule cannot be defended without self-defeat. Defend it, and the defense is an argument offered for outside assessment — which concedes that the claimant's verdicts answer to something beyond the claimant after all, and the reservation collapses. Decline to defend it, and the claim to intellectual honesty stands as a bid for assent issued by someone who exempts themselves from the one thing that could earn assent; it has been announced, not held answerable to reasons. 

And the exemption re-describes everything else, exactly as §3.5 says: a standing that settles when objections land on *this* claim was equally available for every other claim the claimant holds, so the record of concessions and revisions offered as evidence of answerability was conducted, all along, on terms the claimant could suspend at will. Nor is there a moderate version of any of this. One either retains final authority over the verdicts on one's own conduct or one does not; there is no partial reservation, which is why the failure is categorical rather than a matter of degree. 

This is a fact about the structure of the claim, whoever makes it and however skilled they are; it attaches to the assertion, not to the person, and §6.4 takes up why no claimant has been at fault for it. Note also what it does not touch. A person who reflects carefully and claims nothing, even to oneself, has self-certified nothing, and so has not self-defeated. Nor, on the account of §3.6, has that person practiced intellectual honesty: they have done what can be done alone, which is to try. Not practicing and failing at the practice are different conditions, and the argument accounts for both. A failure mode is categorical and detectable (§3.5). Not practicing is simply the condition of everyone before an adequate check exists, and it is no one's fault. 

What is required then is an external check capable of detecting at least the three distinct failure modes (evasion, unresponsiveness, inconsistency), each of which involves not a shortfall in degree but the adoption of a rule that cannot be defended and which could have easily been avoided. And the self-defeating result adds a constraint on the check itself. What defeated self-certification was not who made the claim but how the verdict originated; a check that merely hands that verdict to some other single party puts the administrator in the claimant's position with respect to their own rulings — now they are the authority. §5.3 derives the authority constraint properly; §4 will find the constraint violated in a patterned way. 

The natural reply is that we are not without external checks. Peer review, adversarial legal procedure, and forecasting tournaments all subject claims to external examination and impose costs for failure. If any of these supplies what §3 has shown self-report cannot — a warranted attribution of intellectual honesty — the problem is solved and the argument ends here. §4 examines them. 

# 4. Whether we already have what is needed

## 4.1 The objection to the gap 

Before asking what an adequate check of intellectual honesty would look like, I should answer an objection that any reader of the preceding section might well have formed. Namely, that we possess several such checks, that they are among the most successful epistemic and trust-building institutions our civilization has produced, and that the difficulty I have described was solved long ago. 

The objection deserves to be represented at full strength. Peer review subjects claims to hostile scrutiny by experts who have both the competence to find fault and, frequently, the motive. Adversarial legal procedure is built on the premise that truth is best approached through opposed advocacy under rules that neither side administers. Forecasting tournaments do something neither of the others attempts: they score people against reality itself directly, and the score is a number that (usually) no one can argue with. If any of these supplies warranted attributions of intellectual honesty, then what I have identified is a problem about individuals speaking loosely and simply not availing themselves of tools that already exist, not a gap in our epistemic institutions, and this paper can stop here. 

I want to be clear about what follows. My claim is not that these institutions fail. Each is excellent or at least adequate at what it was built for, and each supplies a genuine, external, adversarial (failure-sensitive; not under the claimant’s control) check on *something*. My claim is that not one of them was built for this — for making warranted attributions of intellectual honesty. And more usefully, that they fall short in a patterned way. For the off-label purpose of enabling warranted attributions of intellectual honesty, each fails on a different property, and the properties they fail on turn out not to be independent of one another. It is that pattern, rather than any individual verdict, that I want the reader to take from this section. 

## 4.2 Peer review

Peer review supplies a real external check by parties with a real interest in finding fault in the claims made. On the first two things one would want from such a check — that it come from outside, and that whoever administers it be motivated to find something — it performs about as well as any institution we have.[^lee]

The difficulty is elsewhere, and it is a difference of target rather than of quality.[^hb] Peer review asks whether *this* paper survives scrutiny. It never asks whether *you* revised. 

Alongside that difference of target sit three further limitations, and I want to draw attention to the relation between them rather than to the items themselves. All three follow from one fact: peer review is administered. Editors determine what is sent for review, what is published, and whether an appeal is entertained. Given that, the journal's remit reserves authority in an editorial board, which makes certain claims structurally unactionable. There is no procedure by which a winning argument against peer review or the claims of the reviewer obligates peer review to yield to some alternative, and there is no inherent cost to ignoring one. And the relation runs in one direction only: a reviewer's demands of a paper are not themselves subject to *symmetric* review. An appeal to the editorial board is itself an asymmetric relation. Finally, certain claims (i.e., papers) can be unilaterally ruled inadmissible as outside of the journal's scope. 

I have listed those three as though they were separate findings. They are not. Each is an *exemption* — a region placed outside the check, a set of mechanics placed outside the check, a party structurally placed outside the same check. All three trace to the fact that someone is administering the check, in an asymmetric position to exercise discretion. I will leave the observation at that here; §5.3 arrives at the same point from a different direction, and I would rather the reader recognize it there than be told it twice. 

And concerning intellectual honesty, there is also nothing to read from the check. A publication record is not a determinate status about anyone's holistic epistemic conduct. It is a count of surviving papers, and it is compatible with qualitatively different underlying practices in the domain of a scholar’s expertise, and especially outside it, where there is no such publication record. Here again the shortfall is not a flaw but an absence of design intent: nothing in the institution was ever meant to produce such a status.[^wiki]

## 4.3 Adversarial legal procedure

Adversarial legal procedure is the most developed adversarial machinery in existence: opposed advocacy, rules of evidence, and a determinate verdict about what is most warranted by the evidence. That last one supplies something peer review does not, at least not as clearly — a determinate, readable outcome. The verdict is not a matter of interpretation. 

But the institution is organized around allocating liability under procedural constraints around what claims have justiciability, not around general inaccuracy as such, and certainly not around anyone's revision or responsiveness to evidence. A trial produces a verdict; it does not produce an update. And the losing advocate does not revise her position; she appeals. Nowhere in the institution is there a measure of whether any participant moved in response to what was shown to them. 

A second difficulty is more instructive. Advocates argue positions they need not hold. It would be wrong to call this a problem of insincerity: the assignment is public and no one is deceived. The problem is structural. There is no declared position of the advocate's own for a status to be about. Whatever a trial measures about counsel, it is her skill rather than her honesty — not because she is dishonest, but because her own view never entered the record. This matters in §5: a determinate status about a person's epistemic conduct presupposes that the person's own position is in the record, and no requirement about anyone's inner states is needed to see it. 

The institution is also administered — judge and jury — and its scope limits (justiciability, standing, jurisdiction) are set by the same authority, constrained by a procedure that the authority itself administers. One concession, because it complicates the picture usefully: procedural rules in law *are* adjudicable within the system. Courts rule on the rules, and precedent can be overturned. That is partial self-correction, arguably more than peer review manages, and it shows that these properties come apart — an institution can possess some and lack others, which is what makes them independent requirements rather than one package under different descriptions. 

## 4.4 Forecasting tournaments

Forecasting tournaments are in some ways the closest existing case to satisfying the conditions for a warranted attribution, but perhaps furthest in another.[^tet] The result is objectively readable — a number requiring no interpretation. It is graded. It is responsive: the score moves when you were wrong, and nothing you do can stop it moving. And the check is external in an unusually strong sense, since reality supplies the verdict directly. 

It is tempting to conclude that there is no arbiter here at all. That is not entirely true, and the truth is more useful. Consider a question asking whether a central bank's policy rate will fall below three per cent by some date, with resolution criteria published in advance that read the rate from the bank's announced *point* target. Several hundred people forecast. The bank then abandons point targets for a *range*, and the criteria say nothing about ranges. Someone has to rule on what the question was asking, and an administrator does. 

So arbitration has not been eliminated. What has changed is *when* it occurs and *whether it can be aimed*. When the criteria were written, their author did not know a range would be adopted, did not know how it would sit relative to the threshold, and did not know which forecasters would exist or which side any would take. The discretion was exercised behind a veil, and a biased author could not have made the bias effective, because the information needed to aim it did not yet exist. The untargetability is structural rather than moral. Compare the editor deciding whether to send a paper out for review, who knows the claim, the author, the institution, and often the likely reviewers. The same act of discretion is aimable in one case and not the other. 

The administrator nonetheless remains present: ruling on ambiguous resolutions, choosing the resolution convention, and deciding which questions exist at all. The last bears on the limitation forecasting is usually said to have, *scope*. A tournament reaches only claims that resolve within some horizon; theological, moral, historical, causal claims and most political claims lie outside it, because forecasting reality cannot score a claim it never adjudicates. That is a limit of the world, and it falls exactly where the need is greatest, since the claims on which straightforwardly-readable reality is silent are the ones that become bound up with identity. But the set of questions actually asked is chosen by whoever authors them, which is scope bounded by authority in the ordinary way. Both limits are real; only the first is unavoidable. 

This forces a concession: adversarial *persons* are not the only possible enforcers; where reality renders verdicts, scoring does the same work, assuming you’ve thrown your hat in the forecasting ring. §5.3's second condition has to be stated so as to admit this.

| Mechanics contestable from within  | ✗ *(peer review isn’t designed to correct itself)*          | partial *(precedent)*                              | ✗ *(conventions, question selection)*  |
| Reach                              | all falsifiable scholarly claims                            | justiciable claims                                 | near-term, cleanly resolvable claims   |
| Boundary authored and aimable      | yes *(remit, desk rejection)*                               | yes, rule-constrained *(justiciability, standing)* | partly; ex ante *(question selection)* |
| **Status about the person**        | ✗                                                           | ✗                                                  | *calibration, not honesty*             |

Three things emerge from the table, and the first two run in opposite directions. 

First, the exemption-type shortfalls do not merely cluster; they *scale*. Where an authority administers a check, three shortfalls appear: 

1. The edge of the check (the boundary line between what the institution will and won't examine) is set by that authority and can be aimed at particular claims
2. The check's own mechanics are placed outside it
3. The check runs in one direction only. 

Each appears in proportion to how much discretion the authority exercises over identified parties and claims. Peer review, where discretion is heaviest and most aimable, shows all three in full. Adversarial legal procedure, whose rules the courts can revisit, shows partial self-correction. Forecasting, where discretion is lightest and usually exercised before anyone can be identified, shows all three attenuated. For example, the one boundary that remains authored, question selection, is set before anyone can be identified. 

The amount of each shortfall tracking the amount of discretion is the pattern a dose-response relationship would show (Hill 1965), and it is better evidence than co-occurrence alone, since it is what one would expect only if discretion were the cause. Three institutions cannot *establish* a dose-response relationship, however. They can exhibit its shape, and that is all I claim. The mechanism is that each shortfall is an *exemption*, and an exemption has to be granted by someone with the standing to grant it.[^platforms] 

Second, *reach* runs the other way, and this is the finding I would ask the reader to hold onto. Peer review can be brought to bear on any falsifiable scholarly claim, which is nearly the whole of what can be objectively known about causality, the human sphere included. Adversarial law reaches only justiciable claims. Forecasting reaches only claims that resolve soon and cleanly, which excludes moral, theological, historical, and most scholarly and political claims on which reality's verdict is complex, slow, absent or contested. So the institution that is nearly free of an arbiter has the narrowest reach, and the one with the widest reach has the heaviest arbiter. That is not a coincidence but a trade. Peer review buys its reach with human adjudicators, and human adjudicators are where discretion lives. Forecasting buys its near arbiter-freedom by letting reality adjudicate, and reality adjudicates only where it is quick and unambiguous. No existing institution has both unbounded reach on objective claims and freedom from an arbiter, and a warranted attribution of intellectual honesty needs both, since there is no domain-limited intellectual honesty to attribute (§3.5). What would be required for unbounded scope is adversarial enforcement from humans without a human arbiter. Nothing we have is built that way, and §5 will say what would have to be true of something that was. 

Third, whatever each institution reads out, it is not about the person’s epistemic conduct. Peer review yields a status about *papers*, and even that only in outline, since the reviews are confidential and the rejections invisible, so that no one's $k/n$ of §3.3 could easily be computed from a publication record. Adversarial procedure yields a status about *cases*. Forecasting yields one about the person’s *calibration* — a real property of a person, but not the one at issue, since a well-calibrated forecaster may be entirely closed on everything her tournament does not score. Not one of these institutions was built to answer *is this person practicing intellectual honesty?* — perhaps a large part of why the answer has never been available. 

In summary, §4's findings suggest that an adequate check would need to be external, adversarial (both of which are satisfied by the institutions surveyed), keep a readable record about the participant's epistemic conduct, and be arbiter-free. This last condition would require an unbounded epistemic scope, that the check's own mechanics not be exempt, and recursive application to the checker's own demands and assumptions, not only to the claims adjudicated.

If none of these institutions supplies what is needed for the purpose of making warranted attributions of intellectual honesty, the question is what would. A specification assembled by patching their gaps would inherit their assumptions, so §5 derives its requirements from the diagnosis in §3 instead, and arrives by an independent route at a set of conditions the reader can compare with those listed above. 

# 5. What an adequate check would have to be

## 5.1 The question, and how I propose to answer it

The two preceding sections were negative. Self-report cannot supply evidence of intellectual honesty and is self-defeating when offered as warranted, even to oneself, and the institutions we have supply real checks that nonetheless don’t supply warrant for attributions of intellectual honesty in a patterned way.

What would an adequate check look like? I want to answer in a particular way and be explicit about the method, because the alternative is a list of desirable properties assembled by intuition, and such lists are worth little. Each condition below is derived from the *diagnosis*, and each by the same move. §3 established that self-report fails because the signal is equally *available* whether or not the state obtains: nothing about producing it requires the conduct it reports. A remedy can fail the same way one level up: the check can be structured so that *it too* passes regardless of the state. Each condition below thus forecloses one such relocation. I have found four: the check may be **self-supplied**; the checker may be **indifferent** to what it finds; the record may be **too interpretable** or it may be determinate about something other than the participant; or an **authority** may decide epistemic outcomes. A fifth relocation would require a fifth condition, and I would want to know of such a relocation. 

By way of review, §3.2 described a particular case of the general problem where a verdict on someone's epistemic conduct comes out the same whether or not the conduct occurred. That gives the reader a test, and I ask that it be applied to every condition below: does dropping this condition let the general problem back in? If not, the condition does not belong here, whatever else recommends it. 

## 5.2 What makes a check informative

Two general points first. The first was made in §3.2 and needs only recalling: what sustains an informative signal is not that honest participants pay a high price but that *deviation* is costly. The conditions below therefore describe a structure nearly free to those already willing to do the thing and expensive only to defect from. A check that imposed heavy costs on honest participants would be the wrong shape; it would measure endurance rather than honesty. 

The second marks the limit of the signaling frame, because the conditions do not describe a signal in Spence's (1973) sense, and a reader who carries that picture into 5.3 will misread them. In the Spence signaling model the state is private and permanently unobservable, and a separate visible act licenses an inference across the gap by its *cost*, not its content. What follows differs in three ways: 

1. The information lies in *content*: what a participant says when a particular objection is put to them, where a different response at identical cost changes the inference entirely. 
2. The examination is *generated adversarially*: a counterparty chooses which claims to press, and is naturally motivated to press where the participant seems likeliest to refuse to practice intellectual honesty — a severe test, in Mayo's (1996) sense. 
3. The constraint on anyone who would hold the status without doing the thing is not price but *feasibility*: some responses are unavailable for purchase at any cost, since producing one would require having done the work it reports. I.e., there is no way to buy a record showing that you conceded an objection (or adequately engaged and answered it) other than by conceding it (or adequately answering it). Conceding selectively does not get around this, since the counterparty chooses what to press, so a record that looks good under that pressure is a record of conceding what one least wanted to. Nor does private insincerity, since a declared credence is a commitment the record then holds one to, and the status is about conduct rather than inner states. 

The barrier to faking is that faking well *is indistinguishable from doing the thing*. That places the structure nearer to the literature on verification than to costly signaling,[^verif] and it means no separating equilibrium across a population is needed. 

One consequence is worth stating, because it discharges the promise made to the dispositional reader in §1. On the practice account, the record is not evidence for a hidden state; the conduct it records is what the practice consists in. On a dispositional account, the same record is evidence from which a trait may be inferred, as conduct is ordinarily evidence of character. The conditions in §5.3 are identical either way. None requires anything of anyone's inner states, and where an inference to a disposition is drawn, it is the reader who draws it and nothing in the system does. The disagreement is real and I have not settled it. It does not need settling for the argument to go through. 

## 5.3 Four conditions

**(a) The check must be external** — not supplied by the person whose intellectual honesty is at issue. This follows directly from §3. The constitutive step of the practice is recognizing when an objection has landed, by evaluating arguments only on their merit. That is the step most exposed to motivated reasoning. A person grading their own intellectual honesty is performing a self-defeating version of the practice where its hardest component is self-assessed by the party with the largest stake in the result. 

**(b) The check must be failure-sensitive, and the claimant must not control which tests are run.** I will go on calling this *adversarial*, but the word names the usual source of the property rather than the property itself. What §3's reason requires is that the test be one the claimant would fail if the conduct were absent, and that she not choose which tests she faces. This is the second maxim of natural justice (§1), *audi alteram partem*, in epistemic form: the other side must be heard, and heard on ground of its own choosing. A check lacking either fails for §3's reason exactly: if it would pass whether or not the person is practicing intellectual honesty, observing that it passed tells us nothing. Now ask where failure-sensitivity can come from. Reality supplies it wherever it renders a verdict, which is why a forecasting tournament needs no human opponent (§4.4). A protocol supplies it wherever the failure can be written into the protocol in advance, which is why an indifferent auditor following one can catch fraud. But for open-ended claims, where reality's verdict is complex, slow, absent, or itself contested, and where no one could specify the failure ahead of time, the only remaining source of failure-sensitive selection is a party motivated to find the failure. That is where persons are needed, and it is where the claims at issue overwhelmingly live, and this is not by coincidence. The principle is Madison's: "This policy of supplying \[compensating for\], by opposite and rival interests, the defect \[dearth\] of better motives, might be traced through the whole system of human affairs, private as well as public," or more succinctly, "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition" (Madison 1788). Of course, his rival interests still answer to authorities of their own, so the principle reflects this condition and not (d). 

It will be objected that (b) makes (a) redundant. They come apart, narrowly. Someone playing genuine devil's advocate against her own position has something like the adversarial interest while failing externality, and she fails for the §3 reason, since it is still she who decides whether her own objection landed. 

**(c) The record must be objectively readable** — it must exist, and it must yield a determinate status about the participant's own epistemic conduct, with no third party exercising interpretive discretion over what that status is. Two things are asked for. A record whose meaning is contested is not yet a check: a transcript is inspectable, but whether someone conceded (or adequately engaged) in it is interpretable, and a status available either way carries no more information than the self-attribution it was meant to replace. This is the third appearance of the same failure. I do not claim that interpretation can be eliminated; nearly every step in an exchange requires it.[^vantage] The condition concerns *where* it happens. Whether an objection landed, whether a distinction is legitimate, whether two claims are matched, are questions the parties put to each other as contestable claims inside the record, and the status is then computed from what neither could coherently defend rejecting (§5.6). Interpretation is relocated into the parties' own claims, where it is checked like anything else, rather than exercised by a reader over the result.[^dialogue] The nearest proposal in the literature makes the contrast plain. Goldman (2001, 95–96) suggests that a novice can choose between rival experts by "dialectical superiority": noticing which one rebuts every piece of the other's evidence while her own goes unrebutted, without assessing the evidence itself. That may or may not sometimes seem close to what a conforming check shows; more certain is that they are not the same thing. In Goldman's case it is the novice's own inference from a witnessed exchange, exposed, as he acknowledges, to polish and coaching. And dialectical superiority can indicate superior expertise rather than conduct. For a conforming check, inference about conduct must be obviated by the straightforwardness of the record. 

And a record can be perfectly determinate and still be about the wrong thing, as §4 found three times. Note what follows: you cannot have a determinate status about someone's epistemic conduct on a claim unless their own position on that claim is in the record. This is why assigned-side advocacy cannot serve, however skilled, and no appeal to the advocate's sincerity is required or wanted. 

**(d) There must be no arbiter** — no party with discretion over *identified* claims or participants; the check must have a terminating replacement, and the rules standing in the arbiter's place must themselves be subject to the check. Suppose the check is administered by an authority who determines whether objections have landed. A ruling that an objection failed is about an argument, not about a person; but it *settles what the attribution will be*, since whether the participant's non-revision counts as unresponsiveness depends on it. So every status downstream of the ruling rests on the ruling. By (a) an attribution requires an external check to be warranted; if nothing checks the arbiter, the status is self-defeatingly certified at one remove. This is the original problem relocated one level up and dressed in institutional clothing. 

I say '*identified* claims' deliberately, because without it the condition is unsatisfiable: every check is constituted by someone, and rule-setting cannot be eliminated, only disciplined. The discipline has two parts. Rules must be authored where they cannot be aimed — before the parties and claims they govern exist, which is the position the question-author in a tournament already occupies (§4.4). And they must remain challengeable afterward, with a winning argument obligating change, since a rule exempt from the system it governs is not only a self-defeating exemption, but an arbiter in written form. The rule-author, unlike the arbiter, makes no attribution about anyone; at authoring time there is no one to make one about. The who-checks-the-arbiter regress bites where discretion touches identified persons, and only there. 

To illustrate, an arbiter rules that Smith's objection didn't land. That ruling is an attribution about Smith's epistemic conduct. But by (a), an attribution needs an external check to be warranted. The attribution about Smith is external to Smith, sure, but what about the validity of the arbiter's ruling? It also needs a checker — external to the arbiter. But so does that checker's ruling, and so on. The regress doesn't stop. No such regress bites properly disciplined rule-setting, provided that the rule's later application to Smith is mechanical and is itself a claim either party may challenge; otherwise discretion has simply returned one step later, as it did for the self-authored criteria of §3.3. 

It will be said that this proves too much: that what the argument forbids is *unreviewable* authority, and that *a reviewable evaluator*, one who gives public reasons and can be corrected, is a different thing. Maybe in practice the regress can be addressed to a *reasonable* degree. Before the structural reply, the reason there is no such thing as a *constrained or reviewable* authority in a check for intellectual honesty should be stated directly, because it comes from the practice's own premise: 
- Let **an authority** be a party whose ruling closes a dispute independently of the ruled-against party's surviving reasons. The practice of intellectual honesty is holding one's claims answerable to coherently defensible reasons. Now take any question of the form *did this objection land?* Either coherently defensible reasons suffice to fix the answer, or they do not. 

If they suffice, *an authority blessing those reasons adds nothing*: any ruling it issues either coincides with what the reasons fix, and is redundant, or departs from it, and is fiat against reasons. If reasons do not suffice, there is nothing on that question for the practice to be answerable *to*, and invoking an authority is not a way of practicing intellectual honesty there; it is a declaration that the practice has no content there. 

**Either way authority is idle or a negation of the premise**, and it cannot be a constituent of a check *for* the practice. *Whether the authority cites reasons is beside the point*; *what matters for the check is the mechanics of how the dispute closes*. 

One thing follows that a reader may want to check. Where reasons demonstrably do not suffice, in the deep disagreements of §7.2, the practice returns a *located* parting point and attributes no failure in intellectual honesty, which is exactly what this argument predicts; an authority there *would replace location with fiat, not complete the practice*. 

The general claim that reasons without an authority’s ruling never suffice to fix the answer of whether an objection lands cannot be coherently argued, since arguing it offers a reason, thus instantiating the very standard the general claim denies. Arguing it from the position of authority, using reason plus the authority to get around the performative contradiction lands one in the pernicious corner of premise-circularity, where the legitimacy of authority has to be assumed from the outset. The insufficiency horn of the dilemma thus survives only in its local form: reasons may very well not suffice to fix the answer of whether an objection lands in particular cases. The local form, however, would need to be shown case by case. 

The structural reply to the reviewable evaluator objection is now a dichotomy, and it is what makes the arbiter-free condition necessary rather than merely attractive. Consider any evaluator who rules on whether an objection landed. Either her ruling can be overturned by a surviving argument from the party ruled against, or it cannot. If it can, she has no closing power: her ruling is a claim in the record like anyone else's, and she is a participant, not an arbiter, which (d) permits. If her ruling cannot be overturned (or an appeals court’s ruling can’t be overturned), the dispute was closed by something other than the reasons in it — regardless of the reasons she gives for her ruling. It's about the mechanics of it: it was the evaluator’s authority that decided the issue, not the reasons themselves. Every epistemic conduct status downstream rests on the acceptance of that closure. There is no third case, because "accountable" means one of these two things: accountable to the parties' reasons, which is the first horn of the dilemma, or accountable to a further authority. Tiered or mutual arbiters, a quorum checking a higher tier, do not escape it; a quorum is an arbiter distributed across a committee, and the regress bites wherever any member exercises discretion over an identified person. 

So the test of §5.1 — to see if removing a condition readmits the general problem of a verdict on epistemic conduct coming out the same whether the conduct occurred or not — holds for (d) universally. It doesn't hold by exhibiting one defective administered system but by showing that *every* system with an arbiter closes disputes by something other than reasons. The reader may ask, what could stand in the arbiter's place? Not a better judge but the parties' own concessions, made on the record by people visibly trying to find defenses that survive scrutiny. 

It does not follow that arbiter-removal alone will suffice, however. Without an arbiter the regress runs the other way, indefinitely, and an unterminated regress yields no warrant either. One party answers the other party’s argument, then the first party answers back, then on and on indefinitely. Or so one might assume based on the way some online disagreements play out. So the condition asks three things at once: no discretion over identified claims or participants, no rule outside the check, **and disputes must terminate without fiat**. They must terminate either in resolution between the parties, at principles neither can coherently reject (e.g., a **retortional layer**), or in a located parting point of the kind §7.2 describes, with evidential weight propagating from whatever is resolved to a determinate result on the original question. What is forbidden is not that a dispute remain open. It is that any arbiter close it. This is a procedural form of what Habermas described: "In discourse what is called the *force* of the better argument is wholly unforced" (Habermas 1990, 160). Nothing provisionally resolves a dispute but reasons the parties themselves concede. Two differences matter. For Habermas, a discourse in which nothing but the better argument moves anyone is an idealized presupposition that actual discourse can at best approximate (Habermas 1990, 91–92). (d) is instead a condition an actual procedure can meet: no party's say-so settles what the record shows. It compels no one to accept the better argument; it simply records whether they answered it. And Habermas's ideal aims at consensus, whereas (d) is good for that purpose but accepts a located parting point as a result (see §7.2 on deep disagreements). 

But what about the feasibility of applying the concept of resolution to the check’s procedural rules? A reader will ask why procedural disputes should have a stopping point that substantive ones can sometimes lack, since §7.2 concedes that disputes about what counts as evidence can be deep, and *what counts as evidence* sounds like a rule. In this framework it is not one. A standard of evidence is a participant's claim, subject to challenge like any other, and it may be located as deep. The rules of the system are procedural, few, and thin: 
- That a challenge must be answered or it stands
	- (In the sense that its *stated* evidential weight enters the record as un-refuted and the failure to answer is recorded, never in the sense that silence confers merit the un-refuted argument did not carry)
- That a stated credence is the belief one holds answerable to reason
- That a standard invoked against another must be stated so that it could in principle be turned back on the one invoking it, otherwise it licenses an inconsistent standard 

Each has the same form. Deny it, and you have licensed one of §3.4's failure modes by name: deny that an unanswered challenge stands and you have licensed evasion. That is what gives procedural rules a retortional stopping point that substantive standards need not always have. They are not commitments about the world; they are the minimal conditions under which any commitment about the world can be checked. To reject one retortion-backed procedural rule is to reject being checked. 

## 5.4 Three further necessary properties are consequences, not independent requirements

Four conditions is already a great deal to demand, and a reader would be right to suspect a longer list of padding. Three (admittedly similar) properties one might expect as conditions are not in fact independent conditions. They follow from (d), by one principle: 

> Each is an exemption. An arbiter-free system cannot grant exemptions, because exemption requires an authority empowered to grant it. 

**Scope cannot be bounded, in claims or in challengers.** A standing epistemic domain restriction could, like any rule, be authored before parties and claims exist; (d)'s first clause does not forbid that. What (d)'s second clause demands is that it then survive as a claim: challengeable by any participant, sustained by argument or not at all. And no such boundary can apparently win that argument, because a bounded system yields a bounded status, and a bounded status cannot underwrite the claim at issue — that an individual exempts no claims from answerability to reason. As §3.5 argued, there is no domain-limited intellectual honesty to attribute. 

The requirement has a predecessor in Bartley's pancritical rationalism, which holds every position open to criticism, the commitment to criticism included (Bartley 1984, ch. 5). The difference is where the requirement sits. Bartley characterizes the rationalist as "one who is willing to entertain any position and holds all his positions … open to criticism" (118): a way of holding positions, which only the holder is placed to attest. He saw that the remedy would have to be institutional, asking how "our intellectual life and institutions" could be "arranged so as to expose our beliefs … to maximum criticism" (113), and later calling for "a vast program to develop critical institutions and methods" (Bartley 1990, 240). What he proposed was competition among institutions; how it is settled whether a criticism succeeded he left open. 

Here unbounded scope is a property of the check, and what it removes includes the one authority his account leaves in place: the critic's own verdict on whether a criticism landed. The same holds for challengers. A rule restricting who may challenge would indirectly let someone select the tests a claimant faces, which (b) forbids, and would give its author discretion over identified participants, which (d) forbids. Anyone competent to argue may challenge any claim (cf. Alexy's rule, adopted in Habermas 1990, 89). Exclusion is justifiable only for bad conduct, by a stated rule that is itself open to challenge, never for who someone is. Openness is a matter of the check's validity; whether the participants who actually arrive are representative is a matter of its power (§7.2). 

**The mechanics cannot be exempt (they’re part of the scope).** A designer who exempted the mechanics would be an arbiter for that class of claims, contradicting (d). This consequence is already visible inside (d)'s own statement. That is not redundancy but a relation worth pausing on. Self-correction does not merely follow from (d); it is what makes (d) *satisfiable*, since a system without it has residual rule-authoring accountable to no one. (d) names a constraint; self-correction names the mechanism that makes the constraint satisfiable. 

**The check must apply at every level (every level is part of the scope).** Exempting a level — like an evidence *standard* — requires someone empowered to exempt it, and there is no such person. Like the last, this also runs the other way: recursive application is part of how residual rule-authority is kept inside the check rather than above it. 

Two of these properties are *derived* rather than required. *Comprehensiveness is self-constructing*: participants may try to submit only safe epistemic positions, but an epistemic opponent targets what they take you to be most likely wrong about, which is disproportionately your unexamined and protected material. Adversarial selection is biased toward the unexamined; self-selection is biased away from it. And evidence-standard consistency is derived rather than enforced, as §3.4 anticipated: a demand for rigor is itself a claim, and applied to the demand, (a) and (b) catch it. An objection to an inconsistent evidence standard should ideally be expressible without asserting anything about a given person's hypocrisy, and it can be: it pairs a standards-shaped claim the agent accepts with a specific claim she also accepts that fails that standard. Both referents are general propositions — neither labels the person. This requires that implicit standards be forced explicit when challenged, itself a consequence of the recursion. 

So the specification is shorter than it appears: four conditions, and three consequences that double as enablers; (d) cannot be satisfied without them. That (d) carries so much is worth noticing, and I return to it in 5.7. 

## 5.5 Plurality, and why there can be only one ledger

The foregoing conditions do not select an implementation; they are silent on interface, governance, funding, and scale. They do not, however, permit an unresolved plurality of *verdicts*. Suppose two conforming systems — two systems that each meet the four core conditions independently — returned different statuses for the same claim at a given time. A reader must then choose which to credit, and that choice is adjudicated by neither. *This reinstates the arbiter*, distributed across readers rather than seated in an institution. Further, a shoppable status is not a determinate status, because shopping is an act of unadjudicated authority. What would've been a fulfillment of the four conditions in a single system is now a failure of two of them — (c) and (d) — in two such systems existing concurrently.

What must happen in such a situation? The divergence between the two verdicts is itself an unavoidable claim: that the other system's verdict, or the rule that produced it, is wrong. Unbounded scope and recursive application (5.4) require each system to admit that claim, and (d) requires each to be bound by its outcome. But binding adjudication presupposes a procedure both parties accept, and there are only three candidates: A's, B's, or a third that both accept. The first two subordinate one system to the other; the third is a single procedure. So there is no such thing as two conforming systems adjudicating their divergence at arm's length. Either they share one procedure, or each of them has exempted its own mechanics from the check, which is the one thing (d) forbids, and has done so on nobody's authority but its own. Each is now self-certifying its verdicts. 

Nor may the two persist as a legible deep disagreement about rules, in the way that two participants may persist in one about a first-order claim (§7.2). The termination clause of (d) applies to rules, not to participants' claims. A rule must terminate at principles no party can coherently reject, because a rule that a party coherently defends rejecting and that is held in place anyway is held in place by fiat, and fiat is an arbiter. A participant may hold a contested first-order position without failing the practice; a system may not hold a contested rule without failing (d). 

The same argument reaches the record. Suppose two systems shared a procedure but kept separate records, so that a challenge to a participant's claim in one did not appear in the other. Then the participant chooses where to be seen, and §3.3's denominator has been handed back to the claimant. So the record must be one, and the dialogue over it fully connected: any claim challengeable by anyone, with every challenge landing in the one record. Two independent conforming systems must therefore come to share one procedure and one record, or at least one of them is not conforming, and the record shows which. The party that insists on sharing them is the only party insisting on satisfying the conditions. 

This is a uniqueness result, and I should say what it does and does not claim. It does not claim that there can be only one interface, one operator, or one source of funding; the first sentence of this subsection stands, and a federated system whose records are one queryable whole under one procedure *is* the one ledger, under as many transparent, inspectable operators and interfaces as anyone likes. 

To further clarify the nature of the problem and reinforce what it requires, this is not the situation of two instruments measuring one quantity, where disagreement, if reasonably narrow, is good and only sends us to calibrate against the quantity itself (e.g., two thermometers independently measuring one quantity — temperature). Here the record *is* the quantity: there is no independent fact about whether an objection landed against which two records could be calibrated; two differing records, giving two different statuses of one and the same claim are *two denominators* (§3.3). 

What this result implies is that there can be only one ledger, and that no party owns it in the discretionary sense, since its mechanics are contestable by anyone and its statuses are decided by no one. A monopoly is an authority no one can genuinely challenge; this is the absence of one, through genuine openness to all challengers that never diminishes by virtue of some arguments winning out over and against others over time. A status of a claim with a long and extensive record of evidence already piled into it is open to as many challengers as it was before any evidence bearing on the claim was weighed within the system. 

What the argument leaves open is not whether multiple such systems will by chance emerge or whether their unification is achievable but whether anyone builds a conforming system at all, and how many people use it, which is §7.2's question about power (as opposed to validity). Nor is any of this a foregone conclusion about a norm of belief revision of the kind debated in the peer-disagreement literature, where conciliationism holds that peers who disagree should each move toward the other (Christensen 2007; Elga 2007; for objections to equal weighting, Kelly 2010). The conditions say what makes an *attribution* warranted; they prescribe no deference to anyone. Such prescriptions are claims to be defended and scrutinized like any other. 

With the foregoing clarified, the specification is falsifiable, and I state the falsifier plainly: exhibit either a system satisfying these conditions whose attributions about epistemic conduct are nonetheless unwarranted, or a warranted attribution of intellectual honesty produced without one. 

## 5.6 Representation

The conditions leave open what the status of a truth claim in the record should look like. It could be a binary verdict, *accepted* or *rejected*, or it could be a number between 0 and 1 expressing how much a claim is favored (above 0.5) or disfavored (below 0.5) by evidence surviving the other side's scrutiny — a Bayesian credence. The question deserves a paragraph because the simpler answer is more available than one might think. There is a well-developed formalism, the abstract argumentation frameworks of Dung (1995), in which arguments are nodes, attacks between arguments are edges, and a fixed rule computes from the attack structure alone which arguments stand, which fall, and which are undecided. A record built that way is fully determinate, requires no arbiter, and uses no numbers and no prior probabilities. So condition (c), objective readability, does not by itself require anything continuous; a binary record can be perfectly readable. 

What a two-valued record cannot represent is *degree*, and §3's diagnosis needs degree in two places. The charge of unresponsiveness is that a person's confidence did not move at all after being presented with new evidence carrying a discriminating likelihood ratio (an LR clearly and appreciably ≠ 1). That charge cannot even be stated unless confidence is something that moves by more or less, which a status of *stands* or *falls* is not. And most real evidence strengthens a claim partially, raising or lowering its credibility without settling it. In a two-valued scheme partial support has nowhere to go: it either flips the verdict or is discarded. So the claim I would defend is narrower than "the record must be probabilistic." It is that (c), together with the requirement that evidence of varying strength be representable, requires a graded substrate: statuses that are credences (degrees of confidence), or that behave like them, whether continuous or finely discrete.[^hahn] What the degree-of-confidence substrate buys is not determinacy, which the two-valued scheme already had, but *graded* determinacy: a determinate answer to how far a claim is favored or disfavored by the evidence, and likewise a determinate answer to whether, and how much, a participant's confidence moved. 

At this juncture, what more should be explored and laid out for the reader about such a substrate? Let the status the record assigns a claim be called its **evidential credence**. I should clarify what kind of quantity it is, or should logically be, because a reader will ask. It is *comparative support for a claim against its own negation* — $H$ vs. $\neg H$ — accumulated from the likelihood ratios of the evidence neither party has coherently defended rejecting, starting from an unmoved prior of even odds. The reader would be right to ask, "why even odds?" Because the evidential credence is a ledger, not a forecast, and the ledger must reflect what it purports to reflect, nothing else. This requires that it record only evidence that has been submitted within a conforming system and has survived the other side's scrutiny within that system. And before anything is submitted within such a system, nothing has survived the other side’s scrutiny — with all arbiters removed — regardless of what has been submitted to scrutiny within other existing critical environments adequate for the task of adjudicating that claim. This includes the existing institutions of science and scholarship, no matter how rigorous and legitimate their output before any of it has been introduced and scrutinized in a conforming system. Any other starting point would structurally favor one party before either had argued within a conforming system, and whoever set it would be an arbiter, which (d) forbids.

So even the claim "the Earth is flat" starts at 0.5. That does not say the Earth is as likely flat as round. The evidence is abundant and needs no authority to observe: ships vanish hull-first, the Earth's shadow on the Moon is round, etc. But what that evidence shows has so far been settled only monologically (Habermas 1990, 66), by each person for themselves, or by an arbiter (e.g., peer review) on others' behalf. The round-earther's verdict is surely warranted. The flat-earther's is not. But neither verdict has yet been put to the other side where the outcome is recorded, and until one is, nothing on the record shows anyone which is right. The 0.5 is bookkeeping about the record, not a judgment about the Earth. Until the evidence is put to the other side in a conforming system and survives, the record needed for purposes of gauging epistemic conduct has nothing in it, and a reported status must say so (§7.2): 0.5 with nothing admitted reads as "nothing admitted," never as "a coin flip." What the evidential credence represents then is the view from between the two parties, answerable only to reasons surviving the other side's scrutiny, without the ruling of an arbiter. 

The evidential credence is not a probability distribution over a partition of alternatives. Each claim is scored against its own negation, so the quantity is well-defined for every claim on its own; coherence across logically related claims is not imposed by the arithmetic but produced the way everything else is, by relations between claims being asserted, admitted, and allowed to carry weight. A participant's declared credence, placed on the same odds scale, differs from the evidential credence by exactly the participant's residual prior, so the gap between them is a comparison of like quantities and not a mismatch of kinds. This implies that mere refusal to update is not a successful rejection of the claim, reflected in the one evidential credence for that claim: a refusal with no surviving defense leaves the evidence standing and records the refusal, so that no one can block inquiry by declining to be persuaded. 

So what does a credence gap imply? The difference between a participant's declared credence (the commitment §5.2 described) and the evidential credence becomes an implicit claim, namely, **that the participant holds evidential grounds not yet in the record**. Forced explicit, those grounds lead to one of four fates for the gap (four credence-gap fates): 

**(1) The gap closes by moving the evidential credence**: The grounds enter the record, move the evidential credence, and close the gap. 

**(2) The gap closes by the participant moving their credence**: The grounds are submitted, but they fail under scrutiny, and the gap closes by the participant updating their credence into alignment with the evidential credence.

**(3) The gap persists, revealing unresponsiveness**: The grounds are submitted but fail under scrutiny and the gap persists, leaving nothing behind it. This gap is unresponsiveness in its recorded form, assuming the participant still refuses to move their credence. 

**(4) The gap persists without implying unresponsiveness**: The grounds submitted imply a standard the other party contests and neither can refute, in which case the gap remains on both sides while the evidential credence never settles away from indifference. This is the recorded signature of a deep disagreement, if it persists (§7.2). A persistent gap here is notably not attributable to an epistemic failure mode and should therefore not be reflected as such (see §7.2). 

There is no fifth credence-gap fate. An evidential credence staying near indifference is therefore ambiguous on its own as bearing upon the participant’s epistemic conduct, but, importantly, it is unambiguous on inspection. This reveals two sub-possibilities for an indifferent evidential credence in addition to the possibility that the evidential credence does not remain indifferent (three evidential-credence fates): 

**(A) The evidence is inconclusive**: Where it reflects admitted evidence that balances, a participant's persistently distant credence eventually has nothing left to rest on. This is a case where the evidence is inconclusive, not where there is a deep disagreement. Evidence that is recognized by both parties or which is defensible under the other party’s scrutiny is not a failure to identify what counts as evidence. Depending on the claim, all such evidence could, when combined, conceivably point in neither direction — neither clearly in favor nor against the claim. It could even conceivably remain this way for an indeterminate amount of time. 

**(B) A deep disagreement**: Where it reflects evidence that could not be admitted as discriminating between the standards in dispute, the distance between parties is grounded in the standard under dispute — a sign of a deep disagreement, if it persists in spite of each party’s continued practice of intellectual honesty. 

**(C) The evidential credence does not remain indifferent**: Where it reflects admitted evidence that does not balance, either the claim or its negation is favored over and against its counterpart.

Thus, there are only four credence-gap fates and three evidential-credence fates. 

Combining the two schemas together, one could delineate, say, six general epistemic outcomes overall, if (1) can only be combined with (C), (4) can only be combined with (B), and (2) and (3) each can only be combined with (A) and (C). Additionally, entries involving the participant’s update can be represented in a graded form: a participant can update partially or slightly after one exchange, for example. These possibilities are given mostly for the reader’s curiosity and to render more legible the illustration below. Drawing this or another conclusion about what sets of possibilities — call them “entries” — exist makes little material difference, so long as the general idea about gaps and the idea about indifferent evidential credences are both understood.    

Since a reader is owed more than assurance that these distinctions are directly readable, here is one exchange with four kinds of entry in it, in the abstract. Let $C$ be a claim, $R$ its proponent at a declared credence of 0.85, $S$ its opponent at 0.20, and the evidential credence at even odds before anything is admitted. 

> **Move 1 — a successful objection, followed by revision.** $R$ proposes evidence item $E_1$ with a stated likelihood ratio of 3 to 1. $S$ objects, stating that $E_1$'s source retracted the finding, and supplies the retraction. $R$ cannot answer and concedes. *Record:* $E_1$ rejected by argument; $C$'s *evidential* credence unchanged; $R$ revises to 0.75. $R$'s conduct entry: objection accepted, $R$'s *personal* credence moved. 
> 
> **Move 2 — a legitimate defeater.** $R$ proposes $E_2$ at 3 to 1. $S$ objects that $E_2$ merely restates $E_1$'s data. $R$ answers that $E_2$ comes from an independent observational cohort and shows it. $S$ cannot answer. *Record:* $E_2$ admitted; evidential credence moves to 0.75; $S$'s objection stands as a stated defeater that failed, which is argument, not evasion. 
> 
> **Move 3 — a pending challenge.** $S$ proposes $F_1$, another observational study, against $C$ at 1 to 3. $R$ does not respond within a procedurally predefined window (itself adjudicable) and states no reason. *Record:* $F_1$ standing unanswered (though always open, of course, to being overturned); its stated weight enters and the evidential credence returns to 0.50 — because admitted evidence now balances; $R$'s silence is recorded as a pending non-response, which becomes evasion only if it persists past a longer conduct-evaluation window — also procedurally predefined and adjudicable — without a stated reason. 
> 
> **Move 4 — a standard in open contest.** $S$ asserts the standard *only randomized evidence bears on claims of $C$'s kind*, and proposes it as a claim. $R$ contests it; neither can produce an argument the other cannot coherently defend rejecting, even after multiple new evidence items from either party are submitted. Applied symmetrically, as the third procedural rule of §5.3 requires, the standard reaches $S$'s own $F_1$ as well as $R$'s $E_2$, since both are observational. *Record:* the standard claim (a new claim separate from $C$) sits at its unmoved prior. For $C$ itself, $E_2$ and $F_1$ are both contested rather than refuted, and neither counts at full weight while the standard is in contest. The evidential credence on $C$ is still 0.50, the same number as after Move 3, but now because nothing is admitted at full weight, not because admitted evidence balances. $R$ at 0.75 and $S$ at 0.20 each hold a *grounded* gap, since each has stated what their credence rests on and the grounds for disagreement is now itself under open contest in light of $S$’s evidence-standards claim currently hinging on an indifferent evidential credence. Neither party is clearly unresponsive — at least not yet. On §7.2's account, however, a standards dispute over a claim like $C$ should not stay here under sustained scrutiny; if it did, nothing could be admitted even in principle, and that would make it deep, which, again, is not what §7.2 predicts for claims shaped like $C$. 

Where it currently sits, the record reads, without anyone interpreting it: one objection accepted and acted on (Move 1); one defeater stated and failed (Move 2); one challenge pending against a named party (Move 3); one standard in as-yet unresolved contest, and a claim whose evidential credence is at 0.50 *because nothing could be admitted so far*, not because admitted evidence balanced as it did after Move 3 (Move 4). That last distinction is the one a person's conduct status turns on, and it is read off the combined contents of the record, not solely off the number, so (A) and (B) can be distinguished. 

It bears repeating: if the procedures above seem flawed or arbitrary, that is all the more reason to emphasize the “mechanics-included” property listed in §5.4. And if the illustration remains abstract, that is a reason to test the procedure in a working implementation rather than only on paper, which is the subject of the next section. 

## 5.7 An attempted implementation of the check

A reader who has come this far may wonder whether these conditions were derived or reverse-engineered, since they are unusually specific and seemingly no system out there satisfies them. The author has built a system intended to satisfy these conditions, with the goal that attempted implementation be tested rigorously. It is described elsewhere and is not offered as evidence here. It is only referenced as an opportunity to better understand an *intended* *implementation* of the conditions. The conditions stand or fall on the derivations given, and what I ask the reader to do here is to test *the paper*: apply the §5.1 test to each condition — including, especially, (d), which by the end of §5.4 is carrying more of the specification than any single condition comfortably should. 

# 6. A corollary

>_"Come, come, and sit you down; you shall not budge;_  
>_You go not till I set you up a glass_  
>_Where you may see the inmost part of you."_ — *Hamlet* 3.4.23–25

Everything so far has been diagnostic. Self-report cannot supply evidence of a person's epistemic conduct; the institutions we have supply it for checking other things; and an adequate check of epistemic conduct would have to satisfy four conditions with three further properties following from one of them. Nothing in that necessarily requires anyone to do anything. But a consequence follows not unlike the original one from §3.7, and I think it is the practical point of the paper. 

## 6.1 The result

> Let $S$ be any system satisfying the conditions of §5, available to an agent $A$. If $A$ offers as warranted a claim to practice intellectual honesty while persistently refusing $S$ or any equivalent, the claim is self-defeating, whatever means $A$ offers as its warrant. A defeasible reason for refusing $S$ can make the refusal defensible, but not the claim.

This extends §3.7 from self-certification to every route. Once $S$ is available, any other basis for the claim, whether a field's checks, a reputation, or a record of corrections, is either self-certification or an authority the practice forswears. A check failing (a) is internal to the claimant, who becomes the authority. One failing (b) lets the claimant select the tests, a selection only an authority could license. One failing (c) is open to interpretation, which only an authority could settle. And one failing (d) readmits an authority by definition (§5.3). A claim not offered as warranted is empty here, as in §3.7, and the result says nothing more about it. The result is conditional: if no such system exists, it is vacuous.

Note also that the claim needs no qualifier such as *holistically*. Exempting even one substantive claim from scrutiny appropriate to its kind discloses the terms on which everything else was conducted, and the exempting rule cannot be confined to that claim (§3.5). **There is no such thing as domain-limited intellectual honesty.** This determines what the scholar who says *"my papers are peer-reviewed"* is actually saying (§6.2).

## 6.2 Defeasible reasons to refuse $S$

Four classes of defeasible reasons are identified: 

1. **Cold-start.** *There is nobody on $S$ in my field; participating would subject me to a form, not to enforcement.* This is coherent, but it *expires with adoption*. It is also weaker than it first appears, because entry is itself informative, independent of whether any counterparty materializes: it puts credences on a permanent record that is costly to deviate from, and it arms a mechanism against the claims and conduct status of the person entering. So the reason holds against the claim that *enforcement* is occurring, just not against the claim that a structurally distinct signal was declined. 

2. **Domain coverage.** *My claims fall within a field already covered by external check.* Given §6.1, this does not narrow the claim to intellectual honesty; it *abandons* it, and substitutes a different and perfectly good claim about one's work. Anyone taking this route has conceded the argument rather than answered it, and I do not say that dismissively. It is the honest move, and it is available. It is also what the ordinary locutions track. *An intellectually honest historian* names someone whose professional work is checked by a domain-local institution, and conventionally, it is a true and useful thing to say *about her work*. It is not an attribution *to her*, and this paper's claim is only that it cannot be converted into one. 

3. **Exposure.** Participating puts one's beliefs on a record an employer, a state, or a congregation may use. This is prudentially justified and morally blameless. It defeats the *culpability* of the refusal rather than the *warrant gap* between the claim to intellectual honesty and demonstrable reality. 

4. **Design critique.** An example: *your $S$ fails condition (d), for the following reason*. This is the critique §5.7 invites, and it may be correct. If it is, the system criticized is not an $S$, and for that system the result’s antecedent is unmet. A critique that rests only on the system’s having a creator, however, mistakes authorship for control: a procedure open to unbounded challenge from anyone is answerable to reasons rather than to its author’s discretion, and whether a given system is so is exactly what the critique can test. Either way, design critique is participation at the level of $S$’s mechanics rather than refusal of $S$. It argues for a different attempt at $S$ rather than for none. 

In all four cases, defeasible reasons notwithstanding, the claim to practice intellectual honesty remains unwarranted, so the honest move is to stop making it while continuing to aspire to it. 

## 6.3 What the conjunction result does not say

It does *not* say that everyone must practice intellectual honesty. Indeed, forced intellectual honesty is self-defeating, since practicing it assumes that the epistemic failure modes are a genuine option. It does *not* say that everyone must participate. It does *not* say that refusal alone is indefensible; refusal with a defeasible reason and no claim to intellectual honesty is coherently defensible. It also does *not* say that non-participation is a fault or a flaw of character: refusal alone satisfies no conjunct, and the paper attributes fault to no one (§1, §6.4). Nor does it say that non-participants are intellectually dishonest. Intellectual dishonesty is a detected failure mode (§§3.4–3.5). A person no check has reached has not failed at the practice, but has not practiced it either, which before a conforming system exists is everyone's condition (§3.7). 

Importantly, it does *not* assert that any particular system satisfies §5's conditions. The antecedent is contingent, and in the one case where I have an interest, I have declined to assert that the conditions have been satisfied, clarifying instead that the *intent* is to satisfy the conditions (§5.7), to test this as rigorously as possible, and allow ongoing scrutiny to answer the question. Whether my implementation *does* adequately satisfy the conditions remains to be seen. 

**Falsifier.** The result is a conditional: if a conforming system is available, exhibit an agent who claims to practice intellectual honesty, persistently refuses the available system, and who nonetheless warrants attribution of the conduct claimed. 

## 6.4 The corollary

The result is vacuous while no conforming system exists. I take that to be a feature, and it yields what I think is the paper's most consequential sentence: 

> Before any such system exists, no claimant can be faulted for lacking warrant. After one exists, every claimant in a position to refuse such a system is newly on the hook — not because anyone's conduct changed, but because the option set did. 

**This is a claim about history rather than about character**. It explains why self-certification has been universal *and blameless* for the entire span of human argument, and why that would cease to be true at a datable moment for specific people, determined by the availability of an adequate check rather than by any change in what people are like. 

One refinement, for precision. The blamelessness has two grounds, and they can come apart. Nothing ever made the question salient of whether self-attributions of intellectual honesty offered as warranted are self-defeating — no institution, vocabulary, or practice put it in front of anyone — and even for someone to whom it became salient, no remedy for the missing warrant existed. But one remedy short of a conforming system is always available to a person who has seen the defect: *saying less*. So the position is three-staged: 

1. Before the defect is visible, no fault of any kind. 
2. After it is visible and before a conforming system exists, fault attaches only to offering the claim as warranted, since saying less is always available even though warrant is not. 
3. And after a conforming system exists and where it is available, the result in §6.1. This is how the paper holds both that the claim was always self-defeating (§3.7) and that no claimant was at fault without salience.

## 6.5 What this section is and is not for

I want to close by saying plainly what I am not doing, because the argument above has a shape that invites a reading I would reject. 

**It is not offered as pressure**, and specifically not as pressure to adopt the system I happen to have built. If the argument works, it works against a class of systems characterized by §5's conditions, of which mine is at most one member and possibly not a member at all. That is what the pilot and scrutiny is for, and I have declined to assert its adequacy as warranted in the meantime. 

**It is not offered to make anyone feel culpable.** §6.4 says explicitly that no one has been at fault for the missing warrant, and I mean it in the strong sense: the people who have asserted their own intellectual honesty over the past several thousand years were doing the only thing available to them and natural for the mind to do without salience about the structural defect.  

**It is offered as a deduction**, and nothing more. It follows, or it does not, from premises stated in §§3–5. If it does not follow, I would like to be shown where. That is a more valuable outcome than agreement. 

And people are of course free to reach their own conclusions about whether it holds, and free to decline it whether they judge it unsound or simply do not care either way. That has to be true, or the argument would be refuting itself. An argument that a person cannot defensibly claim a virtue while refusing every check on it would be a strange argument to make by any means other than reasons. 

# 7. Discussion

## 7.1 What has and has not been established

It is worth separating these, because the argument is easy to inflate and I would rather do the deflating myself. 

**Established, as far as I can tell.** That self-report cannot supply evidence of a person's epistemic conduct through the report itself, and would remain non-verifying even among sincere and accurate reporters, since whatever correlation the utterance might have with the conduct would come from the population and not from the claim. That a record of one's own corrections does not repair this, because the claimant supplies both of its terms. That a self-certified claim to intellectual honesty is a counterexample to itself, since it reserves the very adjudicative authority the practice forswears. That three epistemic conduct failure modes would have to be detectable by any adequate check of intellectual honesty. That the institutions we possess supply real external checks whose exemption-type shortfalls scale with administered discretion, whose reach trades against their arbiter-freedom, and none of which yields a status about the person’s epistemic conduct. That four conditions follow from the diagnosis, with three further properties following from or required by one of them. And that once a system satisfying these conditions is available to a claimant, claiming to practice intellectual honesty while refusing the check is self-defeating or empty, depending on whether the claim is offered as warranted. 

**Not established.** That any existing or proposed system satisfies those conditions, including mine. That a conforming system would likely be widely adopted, which is a different question again and one this paper does not raise. And that the specification is proven to be complete: I have argued that each condition is necessary for checking intellectual honesty and that they appear to be sufficient, not that sufficiency has been proven. Identifying a fifth relocation of the problem being addressed does, however, elude me, and the recurring theme behind all the relocations does seem to be the reappearance of authority and/or exemption — of an epistemic “ruler” getting in the way of rationally answerable rules. Perhaps this is one promising avenue for further, more focused theorizing: show where authority emerges in a system satisfying §5’s conditions; and if it does or doesn’t, show whether authority (as defined in §5.3) is the one thing to be structurally and mechanically removed and replaced with answerability to coherently defensible reasons for a check to be sufficient. 

Two qualifications narrow the foregoing. **Not practicing and failing to practice intellectual honesty are each categorical; successful practice is evidential.** For a participant in an available conforming system or a claimant persistently refusing one, a single sustained instance of any epistemic conduct failure mode settles the matter of failure to demonstrate openness (§3.5, §5), including the persistent refuser’s sustained claim to the practice (§6.1). Likewise settled is the matter of not participating in such a system — one either is participating or they are not. However, for a participant, a long record showing none of the three failure modes under sustained “adversarial” attempts to detect otherwise does not *entail* openness; it accumulates as evidence for it the more one is tested. 

Thus, in addition to identifying non-practitioners by process of elimination, a conforming system reports either a failure to practice intellectual honesty, or a cumulative, growing confidence of successful practice — never the reverse. This is a further reason the substrate should represent degrees of confidence as opposed to binary states of assertion. And what such a record shows is narrower than it appears. Any actual record covers the claims that were challenged, not every claim a person holds, while §6 says there is no domain-limited intellectual honesty. There is no conflict, because the record is not a survey. What unbounded scope secures is that any claim *may* be challenged, so what the record shows is that no claim was successfully shielded from answerability to challenge, which is more modest than that everything was examined. And it is narrower in a second way. The record shows that a person answered to the arguments, not that they understood them: someone who moves into alignment with the evidential credence without following the reasoning behind it is answerable, which is what the practice requires, but answerability is not comprehension, and nothing described here certifies the latter.  

## 7.2 Three problems I have not solved

**Validity and power come apart; only the first is addressed here.** The conditions specify what makes a check *capable* of warranting an attribution of intellectual honesty. They say nothing about how well-supported any particular reading is, which depends on how thoroughly the claims in question are being scrutinized. A conforming system with thin participation is a valid instrument with low power. I do not think depth and width of participation should be a fifth condition: it is not derivable from the concept, it is empirical, and no system could satisfy it at inception. It belongs as a stated limit on what the conditions buy, and it means any epistemic status such a system reports about claims (credibility) and persons (conduct) should convey its evidential base, as a statistical estimate carries its interval. 

Two things sit under this limit that are easy to mistake for objections to the conditions. Participation costs — time, exposure, the asymmetry between those with leisure to argue and those without — will shape who shows up, and a record built by an unrepresentative population has low power on exactly the claims that population does not adequately contest. That is a threat to power and representativeness, and §5.2's remark that the structure is nearly free to those already doing the thing concerns the cost of the *signal*, not the cost of participating. It becomes a threat to *validity* in one case, which I concede: if a system scores inability to answer promptly as a conduct failure, it confounds intellectual honesty with available time and verbal facility. So a conforming system must distinguish *pending* from *declined*, must treat "I cannot get to this" as a stated reason, and must let an unanswered challenge enter the record as evidence without letting the silence itself count against anyone until a much longer, notified window has lapsed. Whether an implementation manages this is relevant to its validity and not merely to its power, and it belongs on the pilot's list. Harassment, burden-dumping, and manufactured objection are real hazards for any implementation, and answering them is an implementation's task rather than this paper's; I note only that a standards-shaped challenge answers a class of objections at once, which blunts the crudest form. 

**Some disagreements survive a fully conforming high-powered check — perhaps indefinitely.** As Chang (1997, Introduction) argues, the absence of a common measure of value does not preclude rational comparison. The possibility I illustrate here is narrower: rational comparison may proceed without yielding a mutually acceptable reason to prefer one foundational standard of value over the other. The clearest case I know is between someone who treats individual autonomy as constitutive of what makes a life go well (Raz 1986, ch. 14, esp. 390–395) and someone who treats it as instrumental to welfare, understood as preference satisfaction, and therefore tradeable. Empirical evidence can establish whether autonomy promotes preference satisfaction, but cannot, by itself, establish whether autonomy is constitutive of a good life rather than merely instrumental to it. The disagreement concerns which standard of value should govern the evaluation of the evidence, and neither standard can establish its own authority merely by applying itself. These are Fogelin's (1985) **deep disagreements**. This is not a defect in the check's conditions: no procedure that adjudicates by evidence (including reason itself) can settle an axiomatic dispute about which standard decides what the evidence shows (cf. the "Non-neutrality" premise of the incommensurability problem in Ranalli and Lagewaard 2022b). What a conforming system supplies here is not agreement but *legibility*: the point at which the parties part company is located exactly, and it becomes visible that they part over a standard rather than over an objective, observational claim. 

I take a disagreement to be deep only if it survives both parties practicing intellectual honesty: neither evades, ignores a verdict coming out of rational scrutiny, or rigs the standard, and still the dispute does not move. Ranalli and Lagewaard draw the same line from the other side: "some disagreements are merely persistent without being deep," as when persistence is owed to stubbornness or "an unwillingness to consider each other seriously" (2022a, 4). The definition is narrower than the literature's usage, however, and it excludes some of that literature's paradigm cases. Their reference case, *Conspiracy*, is a dispute over vaccine safety in which one party relies on conspiracy beliefs "as a way of pre-empting any counter-evidence" (2022a, 4). That is an exemption in the sense of §3.5, and a conforming check would record it as one. The persistence is real; the depth is not.

A disagreement that survives scrutiny can end in one of four places, and only the last is deep.
1. It can be **resolved**. 
2. It can be **not yet decided**: the parties share a standard and the evidence has not yet reached the question, as with an untested drug. 
3. It can be **equivalent by construction**: the parties share a standard, but their hypotheses predict the same thing on every checkable consequence, as with property dualism and physicalism about the mind, or a young Earth created with the appearance of age (Gosse 1857). No increase in knowledge separates such hypotheses, but the dispute can still settle, by tie-breakers neither party can refute, and often it does not survive sustained scrutiny at all: a hypothesis made equivalent by adjustment usually pays for the adjustment in parsimony.[^omphalos] 
4. Or it can be **deep**: nothing either party offers is admitted by the other as bearing on the question — i.e., as discriminating between the competing foundational commitments — and nothing could be, however much either learned through honest bilateral scrutiny. The record distinguishes these without anyone ruling on them (§5.6). Bilaterally admitted evidence that balances two competing hypotheses is inconclusive, not deep. Evidence that could not be admitted as discriminating between the foundational commitments — commitments which survive both parties practicing intellectual honesty and facing one another's scrutiny — is the signature of a deep disagreement. The criterion is *epistemic*: it does not require that there be no fact of the matter, only that no evidence could reach one.

This bounds the class from two sides. 

First, a dispute over an **observationally checkable claim**, one on which observation available to both parties bears, cannot be deep, even when it turns on a standard of evidence. Rival standards that predict differently about what both parties can observe cannot both keep succeeding under sustained, honest scrutiny if objective reality does not contradict itself. If each standard is adversarially tested by its track record, the dispute descends to an ordinary one. Ranalli and Lagewaard's *Young Earth* is the clearest case (2022b, 2–3). Marco holds the Bible a reliable guide to the Earth's age; Marie does not; each finds the other's arguments question-begging. But Marco's standard is a claim, and under a conforming check it must be defended. Defended from the Bible itself, it is a source certifying its own reliability: the structure of §§3.2 and 3.7, moved from a person to a text. Defended by the Bible's record on claims both parties can check, it becomes an ordinary empirical question. Marie's standard faces the same demand and meets it differently: radiometric dating, dendrochronology and ice cores are methodologically independent, and they agree. They rest on assumptions, such as constant decay rates, but not on dogmas: each assumption is open to challenge, and challenges to them have been argued on the record. If such challenges were to succeed, an intellectually honest person would stop relying on the assumptions. This is what **the Challenge Principle** requires: "S is justified in believing their fundamental epistemic commitment C only if S can give a reason to believe C in the face of overt challenge" (Lynch, as stated in Ranalli and Lagewaard 2022b, 3). They add that observation both parties share should be what supplies the dispute-independent reasons they initially find missing.[^hinge]

Second, not every **moral foundation** can be a party to a deep disagreement. Two filters apply, and both work by retortion, though the second ends in a prediction:
1. A foundation that rests on **authority** meets the dilemma of §5.3(d). Defended by reasons, it makes the reasons foundational and the authority instrumental, and the question becomes an ordinary one about whether deference produces what it is supposed to; not defended, it is announced, not argued. 
2. A foundation that **excludes** anyone from moral cooperation on grounds of who they are cannot be defended without self-defeat. By *moral cooperation* I mean holding claims about what should be done answerable to reasons, among people with equal moral standing. On that definition, excluding someone from cooperation just is refusing to answer their objections, and retortion blocks it directly. To defend any claim is to address a community of interlocutors from which no one competent to argue is excluded. Otherwise, claims aren't being held answerable to reason and defending is itself undermined. If who put forward the objection can be grounds for excluding the objection from consideration, then we can't say that a given claim has no undefeated objections. This is Apel's "unrestricted communication community," which Habermas holds "can be demonstrated through systematic analysis of performative contradictions" (Habermas 1990, 88). Rejecting that presupposition is no escape, since defending the rejection is itself argument. Who counts as competent is itself a claim, open to challenge like any other, and not something any party may settle on its own say-so.

   Answering is more than replying: the verdict has to bind those who heard it. A defense that has failed on the record, while the rule it defended stays in force, has been heard but not answered. Whoever keeps such a rule in force while claiming it is justified holds an exemption (§3.5), a claim that is not coherently defensible. Whoever keeps it without that claim is exercising power without justification, which the record shows. Retortion delineates what cannot be coherently defended, not what anyone will do. Nor can anyone be removed from the community of justification itself, losing the standing to object and to have the verdict bind, even in principle. Suppose a justification "succeeded" in showing that X is no longer owed answers. Its conclusion would be closed to any future challenge from X. It would be a claim held so that no outcome X could bring would ever require revising it, which is an exemption. This floor holds by retortion.[^forst]

   Above the floor, unequal terms are not ruled out in advance. Children may not vote, and that is not second-class standing, so long as it is coherently defensible. If the boundary is stated, open to challenge, and lapses, such a rule would be answerable. A boundary drawn on a trait its bearer cannot lose never lapses for that person, and for that person it cannot be told apart from an exemption. So the force of "I'm human too, so why am I second-class?" lies in the demand it makes: name the relevant difference. Treating like cases alike, with the burden on whoever draws the distinction, is about as uncontested as anything in ethics. It is also what Habermas derives from the same presuppositions, that a norm of action must be justified to those it governs, as participants, in terms of its consequences for them: "Every person who accepts the universal and necessary communicative presuppositions of argumentative speech and who knows what it means to justify a norm of action implicitly presupposes as valid the principle of universalization" (Habermas 1990, 86).[^ustar] Every candidate difference falls into one of four kinds:
	1. **The trait (e.g., demographic characteristic) itself.** That is an exemption, and it defeats itself.
	2. **Something the person did.** That can justify a restriction, but the restriction is individual and remedial in principle. It is not about demographic identity.
	3. **A capacity or risk that can be checked one person at a time.** Then check the person. Using the group trait instead means declining an available check on the claim that *this* person has it. That is self-certification (§2), so the proxy defeats itself whenever an individual test exists.
	4. **A risk that cannot be checked in individuals.** This is the only residue, and it is small. Even here, second-class moral standing is out of reach. Such standing is a global *status*, while a risk is always *specific*. Proportionality can fit a targeted precaution to a specific risk, but no specific risk fits a demotion across the board, or a demotion in moral standing. The most it could justify is a narrow measure inside equal standing, never a lower standing.

   So the filter for intellectually honest moral foundations has a floor and a test. The floor, by retortion: no one can be removed from those owed answers, with the verdict binding. Above the floor, any unequal term must name a relevant difference that survives the objections of those it burdens. Kinds 1 and 3 defeat themselves, kind 2 is not about identity, and kind 4 cannot reach status. That no kind-4 measure will survive the scrutiny of those it burdens is part of the prediction below, not something retortion establishes.

What kinds of disagreements survive both bounds are disagreements between two *performatively coherent* foundational commitments, each compatible with non-self-limiting cooperation, that still conflict. The best example I know of is autonomy as constitutive of a life going well against preference-satisfaction welfarism. Habermas seems to reach a similar line by another route, separating moral questions, which "can in principle be decided rationally," from evaluative questions of the good life, "accessible to rational discussion only within the unproblematic horizon of a concrete historical form of life" (Habermas 1990, 108). 

I offer the foregoing deep disagreement bound as a prediction, not a definition. A dispute over an observationally checkable claim, or between foundations one of which fails either filter, that stays unresolved under a conforming high-powered check would falsify the prediction. The illustrations do not assume how such disputes end; they show the route by which they descend, and whether they do is what a conforming check would test. The argument of this paper depends only on there being a great many disputes that are not deep and are worth resolving. Disputes about means to shared ends, or about facts that bear on a value, are ordinary even when ordinarily persistent, and the check reaches them. Whether a vaccine is safe is such a question; whether it should be mandated may part at a fundamental value difference that survives the check.

**Shared error is invisible to bilateral scrutiny.** The check catches what at least one party doubts. It cannot catch a false assumption both parties hold, since neither will raise it. This is not the ordinary point that any system is fallible; it is a structural limit on where the check can look, and it means a status can be well-supported and wrong wherever the participants' blind spots coincide. Mutual scrutiny is only as good as the intersection of the parties' blind spots is small.

## 7.3 What the argument amounts to

The claim to intellectual honesty is among the most common a person can make (often implicit), and it has never carried information of its own; asserted as carrying warrant on one's own authority, it undoes itself. That is not a moral failing of the people making it. It is a fact about the instruments available, and the instruments have been missing for as long as there have been claims. 

What I have tried to give is a specification of what would be missing no longer: four conditions, each derived from the diagnosis rather than from any institution, and each answering a way the original problems in epistemic conduct can reappear one level up. 

If the specification is right, it has a consequence — that a person cannot *both* claim to practice intellectual honesty and defensibly refuse an available check on it once such a check exists. 

If it is wrong, I would like to know which premise or condition fails and why. The correction would be worth more than the result. And this is why the question matters either way. No one defends intellectual dishonesty under that name, and §3.2 showed that it does not need to: people of polar opposite dispositions issue an identical claim about their epistemic conduct. The dishonest strategy operates entirely by being mistaken for its opposite. The disguise is what is load-bearing. A check whose signal is not equally available to both would be the first instrument to weaken that disguise structurally, and it would do so more accurately *and* effectively as participation grows (§7.2). How much would follow from that I do not know. If credibility came to be allocated on checked conduct rather than on self-report, group identity, institutional affiliation, or confidence of delivery, I would expect more of what is decided collectively to rest on reasons that survive ongoing challenge. That is a conjecture, and a subject for another paper. 

Either outcome — the specification is right, or it's wrong but there's a correction — is better than the situation that has held up to the present, in which the question "is this person intellectually honest?" cannot productively be asked because there has not been a procedure that could answer it with warrant.

Heesen and Bright (2021), arguing on quite different grounds — epistemic-consequentialist, and toward the abolition of prepublication review — count among its costs the concentration of judgment in editors, "a relatively small group of people with a deciding vote," and in reviewers whose verdicts show little inter-reviewer reliability; relying on community uptake instead, they argue, would make evaluation "a more democratic process" with greater "independence of judgement" (§§3.6, 4.1). Worth noticing for present purposes: their proposed replacement, post-publication community evaluation, would still certify *papers*. The reform debate concerns how claims should be checked; no party to it proposes checking anyone's conduct. That is not a defect; it is the institution doing precisely what it was designed to do. But it has a consequence for the question at hand. A scientist may say, with complete honesty and with an institution to back her, *"my papers are checked."* She cannot say *"I am checked"* — and it is the second of these that a claim to intellectual honesty asserts.

[^wiki]: Wikipedia belongs to the same family and falls short for the same reason. It is adversarial, unusually close to arbiter-free, and maintains a genuine public record of disputes — but what it certifies is *article content*, not contributors. The difference of target again.

[^tet]: Tetlock, Mellers, Rohrbaugh, and Chen (2014) for the tournaments and their scoring.

[^platforms]: Structured argumentation platforms — Kialo and the argument-mapping tradition — belong here as further cases, not near misses. They keep a persistent inspectable record of an exchange, but they are administered by their operators and represent the structure of a debate rather than any status about a participant. See Klein and Iandoli (2008) and Bench-Capon and Dunne (2007). Adversarial collaboration (Mellers, Hertwig and Kahneman 2001) is the nearest thing in scientific practice to a check on *persons*: disputants pre-agree the tests that would move them, and then run them. It certifies the resolution of one dispute, under an arbiter, and its rarity is itself evidence for §2's picture. Lerner and Tetlock (1999) supply the psychology: accountability to an audience whose views are unknown produces self-critical thought, while accountability to a known audience produces conformity, which is the difference between (b) and a friendly review.

[^verif]: Townsend (1979) on costly state verification; Green and Laffont (1986) on partially verifiable information. I draw the analogy only to locate the structure, and claim no result in either literature.

[^dialogue]: The formal dialogue literature (Walton and Krabbe 1995; Prakken 2006) exists because burdens of proof, permissible moves, and termination all have to be represented and governed. I take that as confirming the shape of the requirement rather than as an objection: those are exactly the thin procedural rules (d) requires to be authored ex ante and to remain contestable.

[^hahn]: The view that argument strength is a likelihood ratio, and that informal argument is rationally evaluable in Bayesian terms, is developed by Hahn and Oaksford (2007). The substrate described here is the record-keeping counterpart of that view: what they treat as a norm for evaluating arguments, a conforming system treats as the bookkeeping of what the parties could not reject.

[^hinge]: The strongest reply comes from hinge epistemology: a commitment like Marco's is not held on evidence at all, and so is not answerable to it (see Ranalli and Lagewaard 2022b for the survey). On the present account, a commitment its holder will not defend when challenged is an exemption, whatever its standing for the holder. The check records the refusal; it need not settle whether the commitment is a hinge.

[^omphalos]: Code's reading of the Gosse case fits the structure of §§3.5 and 3.7. Gosse's system "is internally justifying in the sense that no scrutiny from without can be admitted," and he is "quite unaware of his own dogmatism" (Code [1987] 2020, 22, 23). She frames the failure in this paper's own terms: "Intellectual honesty consists in a finely tuned balancing of these two factors" — self and world (20). The Omphalos adjustment carries a further cost. Its defender must treat observation as unreliable about the Earth's age while relying on observation to read the text that dates the Earth, which is an inconsistent standard in the sense of §3.4. It also carries a moral cost: making the Earth look old when it is not is deception, and a world that records events which never happened (light from supernovae that never exploded) cannot be excused as the mere by-product of creating it mature.

[^ustar]: Habermas's own principle (U) requires that "all affected can accept the consequences and the side effects its general observance can be anticipated to have for the satisfaction of everyone's interests" (Habermas 1990, 65). "Accept" does not mean de facto acceptance; valid norms "must *deserve* recognition by all concerned" (65). Framed as the satisfaction of interests, though, (U) risks deciding the autonomy–welfare dispute in welfare's favor before it is heard. A version that does not: *a norm is valid only if a coherent defense of it survives ongoing scrutiny, open to anyone, of the consequences its general observance can be expected to have for everyone it affects, counting what each of them can show, likewise under scrutiny, matters to them.* Its defense is beyond this paper.

[^forst]: The conclusion is Forst's. He holds that there is a "basic right to justification, which may not be denied to any moral person" (Forst 2012, 81), "a right that one cannot have reasons for rejecting" (215), and that it "is and remains irrevocable" (6–7). Much of the route is shared as well. Rejecting the right with reasons would mean offering reasons to the person whose standing to receive them is denied. What differs is ambition rather than structure. Forst reaches the right by "recursive" reflection on what it means to justify a norm, and denies that this means "one has 'always already' accepted moral duties insofar as one is a thinking, reasonable, or arguing human being in a general sense" (214–15). With Habermas, he rejects Apel's identification of "rational commitments of argumentative reason as such" with moral duties (56). Nothing here depends on that identification. The argument is limited to claims: it shows what cannot be coherently defended, including that a norm binding others is justified, and not that anyone will act on what can be defended. What this paper adds is the connection to §3.5. Denying the right is an exemption: its conclusion would be closed to any future challenge from the person denied. So it fails for the same reason any exemption does, and it re-describes the practice of whoever holds it, who is then conducting the whole of their justification under a rule that would let them exempt anyone.
